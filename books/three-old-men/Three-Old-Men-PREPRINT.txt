Preface 
They say that when old men gather, they talk about the past. But that's only 
half true. 
When old men gather—truly old men, the kind who have outlived wars and 
wives and most of their certainties—they talk about everything. They argue about 
the price of rice and the nature of God. They laugh at jokes that would make 
younger people uncomfortable. They cry sometimes, though they'd never admit it 
later. 
This is a story about three such men. 
I won't tell you their real names. They wouldn't have wanted that. What I will 
tell you is that they met every week for decades at the same tea house, at the same 
table by the window where the afternoon light fell just right. They ordered the same 
things—jasmine tea for one, chrysanthemum for another, and plain hot water for 
the third, who always claimed tea was "too much fuss." 
They talked about time and love, about control and suffering, about all the 
things they'd done wrong and the few things they'd done right. They talked about 
death with the casual familiarity of men who could see it approaching like an old 
acquaintance walking down the street. 
This book is not a transcription of their conversations. How could it be? I 
wasn't there for most of them, and memory is a river that changes its course with 
every retelling. 
What this is, instead, is my attempt to capture something true—the way 
three men who had nothing left to prove and nothing left to lose finally said the 
things that mattered. 
The tea house closed three years ago. The table by the window is gone. 
But the conversations continue. 
They always do, if you know how to listen.

Chapter One 
The Nature of Time 
In which three old men gather and discover that clocks lie 
❧ 
 
The tea house sat on the corner of Charoen Krung Road, where it had been 
sitting for sixty-seven years, watching the city grow tall around it like bamboo 
overtaking a garden. It had survived floods and coups and the relentless march of 
progress, and now it remained—stubborn, wooden, slightly crooked—sandwiched 
between a massage parlor and a 7-Eleven that hummed with fluorescent 
indifference. 
Inside, at the table by the window where the afternoon light fell in long golden 
stripes, three old men were having an argument about clocks. 
"The problem with clocks," said Somchai, stirring his jasmine tea with the 
slow deliberation of a man who had nowhere else to be, "is that they lie to us from 
the moment we're born." 
Prasert snorted. He was a compact man with steel-gray hair and the 
permanently skeptical expression of someone who had spent forty years as an 
engineer, fixing other people's mistakes. "Clocks don't lie. Clocks are machines. 
Machines do exactly what they're designed to do." 
 
9 
"And what are they designed to do?" Somchai asked, his weathered face 
crinkling into something that might have been a smile or might have been the 
prelude to a trap. 
"Tell time, obviously." 
"Ah." Somchai lifted his teacup. "But do they? Or do they merely count? Tick, 
tick, tick. One second, then another, then another. They show us numbers 
changing on a dial. But time—real time, the time we actually experience—has 
nothing to do with those numbers." 
"Here we go," muttered Prasert into his chrysanthemum tea. 
The third man at the table—Anong, who was older than the other two by 
nearly a decade and had the dreamy, distant look of someone whose mind 
frequently wandered through the halls of memory—leaned forward with sudden 
interest. 
"You know," Anong said, his voice carrying the slight tremor of age, "I was 
thinking about my granddaughter's wedding last week. Or was it last month? 
Anyway, I was thinking about it, and I remembered my own wedding like it was 
yesterday. Noi in her white dress. The way she looked at me when I forgot my 
vows and had to make something up on the spot." 
He chuckled, and the chuckle turned into a cough, and the cough subsided 
into silence. 
"That was fifty-three years ago," Anong continued. "But in my mind, I can 
touch it. I can smell the jasmine flowers in her hair. I can hear her laugh when I 
called her the wrong name—I was so nervous, you see, I called her by her sister's 
name, and she never let me forget it, not once in forty-eight years of marriage." 
He paused, and his eyes grew distant. 
"Forty-eight years. The clock says it was forty-eight years. But it felt like one 
long afternoon. One beautiful afternoon that ended too soon." 
 
10 
Prasert shifted uncomfortably. He never knew what to do when Anong talked 
about Noi. Five years she had been gone, and still Anong spoke of her in the 
present tense as often as not. 
"What Somchai is trying to say—" Prasert began. 
"I'm not trying to say anything," Somchai interrupted mildly. "I'm saying it. 
The clock is a liar. It tells us that time moves at a constant rate, one second per 
second, steady and reliable. But we know better, don't we? We've lived long enough 
to know." 
He gestured toward the window, where the late afternoon light was turning 
the old wooden frames to gold. 
"When you were young, Prasert—when you were building your bridges and 
your buildings and all those things you were so proud of—did time feel the same 
as it does now?" 
Prasert opened his mouth to argue, then closed it. He thought about the 
twenty-hour days he used to work, the way weeks would blur together in a rush of 
deadlines and concrete and the satisfaction of making something stand where 
nothing had stood before. 
"It was different," he admitted reluctantly. "But that's just perception. The 
actual time—" 
"There is no 'actual' time," Somchai said. "There's only the time we 
experience. And that time expands and contracts like a living thing." 
"Like a heartbeat," Anong said suddenly. "My grandmother used to say that 
time had a heartbeat. When you're happy, it beats fast, rushing to get to the end of 
the joy. When you're sad, it slows down, trying to give you room to feel everything." 
"Your grandmother was very poetic," Somchai said approvingly. 
"Your grandmother was full of nonsense," Prasert said, but not unkindly. "No 
offense to her memory." 
 
11 
"She would have liked you," Anong said, smiling. "She always said she 
trusted a man who argued with her more than a man who agreed too easily." 
❧ 
 
The waiter came by—a young man in his twenties who moved with the 
impatient energy of someone who had many other places he'd rather be. He refilled 
their teapots without asking and disappeared again, his sneakers squeaking against 
the worn wooden floor. 
"He's in a hurry," Anong observed. "I remember being in a hurry. Always 
running to the next thing, the next customer, the next shipment. I owned seventeen 
fishing boats at one point, did I ever tell you?" 
"Several thousand times," Prasert said drily. 
"Seventeen boats, and every single one of them needed something. New nets. 
Engine repairs. Crews who didn't drink too much and show up late. I used to wake 
up at four in the morning and not stop moving until midnight." 
"And now?" Somchai asked. 
Anong laughed—a gentle, self-deprecating sound. "Now I wake up at four in 
the morning because my bladder insists on it, and I move as little as possible. 
Strange, isn't it? I spent sixty years running around like a madman, and what did it 
get me? The same destination as everyone else. An old man at a tea house, waiting." 
"Waiting for what?" Prasert asked. 
It was meant to be a challenge, but Anong took it seriously. He looked out 
the window at the street below, where motorcycles weaved between cars and a fruit 
vendor was setting up her cart with practiced efficiency. 
"I don't know anymore," he said quietly. "I used to wait for things. For 
success. For my children to make me proud. For Noi to look at me the way she 
 
12 
did when we were young." He paused. "Now I think I'm just waiting for the waiting 
to end." 
The silence that followed was the kind that only old friends can share—
weighted with understanding, empty of judgment. 
"That's morbid," Prasert said finally. 
"That's honest," Somchai corrected. 
"Same thing, at our age." 
❧ 
 
Somchai reached into his pocket and produced a small object—a worn pocket 
watch, brass gone green with age, the crystal scratched and cloudy. He placed it on 
the table between them. 
"My father gave me this when I turned twenty," he said. "He said it had 
belonged to his father, and his father before him. Four generations of men, all 
watching the same hands go around and around." 
Prasert leaned forward despite himself. He had known Somchai for nearly 
thirty years, and in all that time, he had never seen the watch. 
"Does it still work?" 
"Oh, it runs." Somchai wound the crown gently, and the watch began to 
tick—a soft, intimate sound, like a whisper in a quiet room. "But it loses about 
three minutes every day. Has since before I was born. My great-grandfather 
apparently dropped it during the war—no one remembers which war—and it's 
never been the same." 
"So it's useless," Prasert said. 
"On the contrary. It's the most honest clock I've ever known." 
 
13 
"Because it's wrong?" 
"Because it admits it's wrong." Somchai picked up the watch and held it to his 
ear, listening to the tick. "Every clock is wrong, Prasert. Every single one. They all 
drift, all lose or gain, all fall slowly out of step with the arbitrary standard we've 
decided to call 'real' time. Most clocks pretend otherwise. They show us their 
numbers with such confidence, such certainty. But this old thing—" he smiled, "—
this old thing knows better. It knows it's just counting, and that the counting means 
nothing." 
"If time means nothing, then what's the point of anything?" Prasert 
demanded. "Why plan? Why build? Why bother?" 
"Ah." Somchai set the watch down again. "Now you're asking the right 
questions." 
❧ 
Anong was staring at the watch with an expression Prasert couldn't quite read. 
"You know what I think about," Anong said slowly, "when I can't sleep at 
night? Which is most nights now—old men don't sleep well, have you noticed?" 
"I've noticed," Prasert said. 
"I think about the time I wasted." Anong's voice was soft, almost wondering. 
"Not the big things—I don't regret working hard or building the business or any 
of that. But the small things. The hours I spent worrying about problems that never 
happened. The days I was angry about things that don't matter anymore. The 
years—years!—I spent thinking about the future instead of noticing the present." 
"That's what young people do," Somchai said gently. "They have to. If they 
didn't think about the future, nothing would ever get built or planted or raised." 
"But they don't know the cost," Anong said. "I didn't know. Nobody told me 
that every minute I spent planning for later was a minute I was giving up from 
now. And now—" he gestured vaguely at himself, at his fragile body, at the liver 
spots on his hands, "—now 'now' is all I have, and I don't have much of it left." 
 
14 
"Nobody knows how much they have left," Prasert said, uncomfortable with 
the direction of the conversation. "That's true at any age." 
"But at our age, we can make reasonable estimates." Anong smiled, and there 
was something both sad and free in the expression. "When I was forty, I could tell 
myself I might live another forty years. It was probably true! But now? If I get five 
more years, I'll be lucky. If I get ten, I'll be amazed. If I get twenty—" he laughed, 
"—I'll start believing in miracles." 
"Or medicine," Prasert muttered. 
"Medicine can give you time," Somchai said. "It can't give you presence. It 
can't give you the ability to actually be in the moments you have left." 
"Is that what you're doing?" Prasert asked, genuinely curious despite his 
skepticism. "Being present?" 
Somchai considered the question with the seriousness it deserved. 
"I'm trying," he said finally. "It's harder than you'd think. After seventy-five 
years of learning to live in the future—planning, hoping, fearing, anticipating—it's 
very difficult to stop. The mind doesn't want to rest. It wants to keep running 
ahead, scouting for danger, looking for opportunity." 
"The mind is a good servant but a bad master," Anong said, and then frowned. 
"Who said that? I'm sure I read it somewhere." 
"Does it matter who said it?" Somchai asked. "The truth is the truth, whether 
it comes from a sage or a street vendor." 
"I'd trust the street vendor more," Prasert said. "At least she's trying to give 
you something useful." 
❧ 
 
 
15 
The light through the window was changing now, the gold deepening to 
amber as the afternoon began its slow surrender to evening. In the street below, 
the fruit vendor had finished setting up and was calling out prices in a melodic 
singsong that drifted up through the old wooden walls. 
"I've been thinking about my mother lately," Somchai said, seemingly out of 
nowhere. 
The other two waited. They had known Somchai long enough to know that 
his apparent non sequiturs usually led somewhere. 
"She died when I was twelve," he continued. "I remember the day very 
clearly—not the whole day, but pieces of it. The way the light came through the 
hospital window. The smell of the antiseptic. The sound of my father crying, which 
I had never heard before and haven't heard since." 
He paused, collecting himself. 
"I'm seventy-five years old now. That means my mother has been dead for 
sixty-three years. Sixty-three years! And yet when I think of her, she's as present as 
either of you. More present, maybe, because I've been carrying her in my heart 
longer than I've known you." 
"That's sweet," Anong said, meaning it. 
"It's strange is what it is," Somchai said. "Sixty-three years, and time hasn't 
touched her at all. She's still thirty-four years old. She still has that smile she used 
to save for just me, the one she gave me when I brought her flowers I'd picked 
from the neighbor's garden. She still tells me to sit up straight and eat my vegetables 
and be kind to people who are unkind to me." 
"The dead don't age," Prasert said. "That's not philosophy—that's just how 
memory works." 
"But what is memory except a form of time travel?" Somchai asked. "When I 
think of my mother, I'm there with her again. Not just remembering—being. For 
just a moment, I'm twelve years old again, and she's alive, and everything is 
possible." 
 
16 
"And then you come back," Prasert said flatly. "And you're seventy-five, and 
she's still dead, and everything is exactly as it was before." 
"Yes," Somchai acknowledged. "But for that moment—that eternal, timeless 
moment—the clock was wrong. Sixty-three years had passed, but also no time at 
all." 
Anong was nodding vigorously. "Yes, yes, that's exactly it. When I think of 
Noi—when I really think of her, not just remember but think—she's right here. I 
can feel her hand in mine. I can hear her telling me I forgot to lock the back door, 
the way she did every night for forty-eight years." 
"You probably did forget," Prasert said. 
"I almost certainly did. But that's not the point." 
"What is the point?" 
Anong looked at him with surprising directness. "The point, old friend, is that 
the people we love don't go away. They can't. They're woven into us. Every time 
we think of them, they're alive again. Every time we hear their voice in our heads 
or feel their presence in a room, they've defeated time. They've come back from 
wherever they went." 
"That's comforting," Prasert said. "It's also nonsense." 
"Why can't it be both?" 
❧ 
 
The waiter returned with fresh hot water for Prasert's plain cup—because 
Prasert, true to form, refused to drink anything so frivolous as tea and insisted on 
hot water like some kind of ascetic monk, though he would have been offended 
by the comparison. 
 
17 
"Thank you," Prasert said gruffly, and the waiter nodded and disappeared 
again. 
"He doesn't know us," Anong observed. "We've been coming here for thirty 
years, and he doesn't know our names or our stories or anything about us." 
"Why should he?" Prasert asked. "We're just three old men taking up a table." 
"We're the whole universe," Somchai said mildly. 
Prasert rolled his eyes. "Here we go again." 
"I mean it. From his perspective, we're background. Furniture. But from our 
perspective, we're the center of everything. Every thought we've ever had, every 
feeling, every experience—it's all here, inside these three creaky old bodies. When 
we die, universes die with us." 
"Cheerful thought over tea." 
"It's not meant to be cheerful or uncheerful. It's just true. And it relates to 
time, which is what we were talking about." 
"Were we? I lost track." 
"We were. And my point is this: the time we experience—the only time that 
matters—is completely internal. The clock on the wall says it's five-thirty in the 
afternoon, and that's true for everyone. But the time inside us—the weight of years, 
the depth of moments, the way certain seconds stretch into eternities while entire 
decades vanish like dreams—that's unique to each of us. Incomparable. 
Incommunicable." 
"If it's incommunicable, why are we talking about it?" Prasert asked, but there 
was no real challenge in his voice anymore. He was, despite himself, interested. 
"Because talking is how we remind ourselves that we're not alone," Somchai 
said. "Even if we can't truly share our inner experience, we can wave at each other 
across the distance. We can say: 'I'm here too. I'm also confused. I'm also running 
out of time.'" 
 
18 
❧ 
Anong had been quiet for a while, watching the street below with an 
expression of peaceful melancholy. Now he turned back to his friends with a 
question that seemed to surprise even him. 
"If you could go back to any moment in your life—not to change anything, 
just to be there again—where would you go?" 
Prasert shifted in his seat. "That's a ridiculous hypothetical." 
"Humor me. I'm old and easily entertained." 
"Fine." Prasert thought about it, his engineer's mind trying to optimize the 
problem. "The day we completed the Siriraj Bridge. 1983. I'd been working on it 
for three years, and when we finally did the load test and everything held—" he 
smiled despite himself, "—I felt like I could conquer anything." 
"Professional achievement," Anong said. "I should have guessed." 
"What about you?" 
Anong didn't hesitate. "The birth of my first son. Not the birth itself—that 
was terrifying, all that blood and screaming. But the moment after, when the nurse 
put him in my arms and he looked at me with those little unfocused eyes, and I 
realized I would die for this tiny creature without a second thought." 
"And you?" Prasert asked Somchai. "What moment would you choose?" 
Somchai was quiet for so long that Prasert wondered if he'd heard the 
question. Finally, he spoke. 
"No moment in particular." 
"That's not an answer." 
"It's the only answer I have." Somchai folded his hands on the table, his long 
fingers intertwined. "I've thought about this question a lot, actually. More than 
 
19 
you'd expect. And I've realized that the moment I would choose isn't a moment at 
all. It's a quality of moments." 
"Meaning?" 
"Meaning there were times in my life when I was fully present. Not thinking 
about the past, not worrying about the future, just... there. Completely there, in my 
body, in the world, awake to everything. Those moments could come at any time—
while teaching a class, while walking home in the rain, while doing nothing at all. 
And if I could go back, I would go back to that quality. That presence. Not to any 
particular scene, but to that way of being." 
"That's very abstract," Prasert said. 
"That's very honest," Anong corrected. 
"Can you tell them apart anymore?" 
❧ 
 
The sun was setting now, painting the sky in shades of orange and pink that 
reflected off the glass towers that had grown up around the tea house like a forest 
of steel and light. The fruit vendor below was packing up her cart, counting her 
money, getting ready for the long motorcycle ride home. 
"We should probably go," Prasert said, though he made no move to rise. 
"Probably," Anong agreed, also not moving. 
"The problem with leaving," Somchai said, "is that we can never come back 
to exactly this moment. This light. This conversation. This feeling of being 
together." 
"We come back every week," Prasert pointed out. 
"We come back to the tea house. We sit at this table. But the moment is always 
different. The river of time has moved on, and we're standing on a different bank." 
 
20 
"You and your rivers." 
"I'm a simple man. I like water metaphors." 
Anong laughed—a warm, genuine sound that made the other two smile 
despite themselves. 
"You know what I love about us?" Anong said. "We've been doing this for 
thirty years, and we still don't agree about anything." 
"We agree about some things," Prasert said. 
"Name one." 
Prasert opened his mouth, closed it, opened it again. "We agree that this is a 
good table." 
"That's not philosophy." 
"Philosophy is overrated. This table has good light and is far enough from the 
kitchen that you can actually have a conversation. That's worth more than all of 
Somchai's abstractions." 
"I'll take that as a compliment," Somchai said serenely. 
"It wasn't meant as one." 
"I know. I'm taking it anyway." 
❧ 
 
Finally, reluctantly, the three old men began the slow process of gathering 
themselves to leave. This was not a simple operation. There were bills to be sorted 
(Prasert always insisted on paying his exact share, down to the satang, while Anong 
always tried to pay for everyone, and Somchai simply let them argue until they wore 
themselves out). There were joints to be carefully tested before putting weight on 
 
21 
them. There were the inevitable trips to the restroom, taken one at a time while the 
others waited with patient understanding. 
"Same time next week?" Anong asked as they shuffled toward the door. 
"Same time next week," Somchai confirmed. 
"Assuming we're all still here," Prasert added, because he liked to keep things 
realistic. 
"We'll be here," Anong said with sudden certainty. "We always are." 
They stepped out into the evening air, which was warm and thick with exhaust 
and cooking smells and the particular scent of Bangkok at dusk—a mixture of 
jasmine and diesel and something indefinably alive. 
"The city has changed," Anong said, looking up at the towers that surrounded 
them. 
"Everything changes," Somchai said. 
"Except us," Prasert said drily. "We've been having the same conversation for 
thirty years." 
"That's not true. The conversation changes. We change. Only the arguing 
stays the same." 
"I'll take that," Prasert said. "Arguments keep you sharp." 
"Arguments keep you stubborn." 
"Same thing, at our age." 
They parted ways at the corner, each heading toward their own home through 
streets they had walked a thousand times before. The night was falling, and the 
neon signs were flickering to life, and the clock on the bank across the street said 
it was 6:47 PM. 
But the clock, as they all knew by now, was lying. 
 
22 
It was always lying. 
The only true time was the time inside them—the weight of years, the warmth 
of friendship, the strange certainty that despite all evidence to the contrary, this 
particular moment would last forever. 
It wouldn't, of course. 
Nothing does. 
But walking home through the Bangkok twilight, each of the three old men 
held onto that feeling anyway—the feeling of having been fully present, just for an 
afternoon, with people who understood. 
The clock could count whatever it wanted. 
This was what time actually felt like. 
 
❧ 
 
End of Chapter One

Chapter Two 
Love and Attachment 
In which they remember the women who shaped them 
❧ 
 
The rain came without warning, as rain does in Bangkok—one moment the 
sky was merely gray, the next it was falling in sheets that turned the streets into 
rivers and sent pedestrians scrambling for cover under awnings and into doorways. 
Inside the tea house, the three old men watched the deluge through the 
window with the calm detachment of people who had nowhere they needed to be. 
"Good day for it," Anong said, meaning the tea, meaning the company, 
meaning this small moment of shelter while the world outside tried to drown 
itself. 
"Good day for staying inside," Prasert agreed, which was as close as he came 
to poetry. 
Somchai simply smiled and poured himself more jasmine tea. 
The rain drummed against the old roof, finding leaks that had been there for 
decades and would likely be there for decades more. A slow drip had started in the 
corner, falling into a bucket that the waiter had placed there with the weary 
expertise of long practice. 
 
24 
"It rained like this the day I met Noi," Anong said, as if the memory had 
surfaced unbidden—which it had, as memories do, triggered by weather and light 
and the particular quality of this moment. 
The other two waited. They had heard this story before, but stories improve 
with repetition, and besides, what else was there to do while the world drowned 
outside? 
"I was twenty-three," Anong continued. "Young and stupid and convinced I 
was neither. I had just taken over my father's fishing business—one boat, held 
together with rope and prayers—and I thought I was the most important man in 
the province." 
"You haven't changed much," Prasert observed. 
"I've gotten humbler. Slightly." Anong smiled at the memory. "Anyway, it was 
raining like this, and I was running through the market trying to find shelter, and I 
crashed right into her. Literally crashed. Sent her vegetables everywhere. Cabbages 
rolling down the street like little green heads." 
"Romantic," Prasert said. 
"It was terrible. She was furious. She called me a water buffalo with feet for 
brains—which, looking back, was fair. And I just stood there, soaking wet, 
watching her pick up her cabbages, and I thought: I'm going to marry that woman." 
"Love at first insult," Somchai said. 
"The best kind. Anyone can fall in love with someone who's nice to you. But 
when someone calls you a water buffalo and you still want to spend your life with 
them—that's when you know it's real." 
"Did you help her pick up the cabbages, at least?" 
"Eventually. After I finished staring like an idiot." Anong's eyes were distant 
now, looking at something the others couldn't see. "She had this way of looking at 
you—like she could see everything you were trying to hide. Forty-eight years of 
 
25 
marriage, and she never lost that look. Right up until the end, she could still see 
through me completely." 
The rain continued its assault on the roof. In the street below, a lone 
motorcyclist was attempting to navigate the flooded road, his engine coughing in 
protest. 
"I didn't marry for love," Prasert said suddenly. 
The other two looked at him with surprise. Prasert rarely talked about his 
wife, who had died fifteen years ago after a long illness that neither of them 
discussed. 
"My parents arranged it," he continued, his voice carefully neutral. "Mei's 
family had land, my family had debts, and that was that. I met her twice before the 
wedding. We barely spoke." 
"But you learned to love her," Anong said. It wasn't a question. 
Prasert was quiet for a long moment. "I learned to... appreciate her. To respect 
her. To rely on her. Is that love? I don't know. We were together for forty-two 
years, and I still don't know." 
"It sounds like love to me," Somchai said gently. 
"That's because you romanticize everything." But there was no bite in 
Prasert's words. "Mei was practical. Sensible. She never expected me to be 
something I wasn't. She never complained when I worked late or forgot 
anniversaries or failed to say the things a husband is supposed to say." 
"What things?" 
Prasert shrugged uncomfortably. "You know. The things in movies. 'I love 
you.' 'You're beautiful.' 'I don't know what I'd do without you.'" 
"Did you never say those things?" 
"Once. At the end. When it didn't matter anymore." Prasert picked up his cup 
of hot water and stared into it. "She was in the hospital, and the doctors had told 
 
26 
me she probably wouldn't make it through the night. And I sat there holding her 
hand, and I said all the things I should have been saying for forty-two years." 
The silence that followed was heavy with things unspoken. 
"What did she say?" Anong asked quietly. 
"She said: 'I know. I always knew.'" Prasert's voice cracked slightly, then 
steadied. "And then she died. Just like that. Between one breath and the next." 
"That's a good way to go," Somchai said. "Knowing you were loved. Knowing 
you had loved." 
"Is it? Sometimes I think I should have said those things earlier. Given her 
years of hearing them instead of one night." 
"The saying isn't the important part," Somchai said. "The living is. You 
showed her love for forty-two years, even if you didn't say it. Every time you came 
home instead of staying at work. Every time you sat with her when she was sick. 
Every time you let her win an argument because her happiness mattered more than 
being right." 
Prasert looked up sharply. "How do you know about that?" 
"Because that's what love looks like. Not grand gestures and poetry. Just 
showing up, day after day, choosing someone over and over again." 
❧ 
 
The rain was beginning to ease, though the streets would stay flooded for 
hours yet. The tea house had filled up with refugees from the storm—a young 
couple sharing an umbrella, a businessman in a wet suit, an old woman with a small 
dog tucked inside her jacket. 
"And you, Somchai?" Anong asked. "You never married. But surely there was 
someone?" 
 
27 
Somchai was quiet for so long that Anong began to regret asking. But finally, 
the old teacher spoke. 
"Her name was Lalita," he said. "We were together for three years, when I 
was teaching in Chiang Mai. She was a nurse at the hospital there—kind, gentle, 
with a laugh that could light up a room." 
"What happened?" 
"Life happened." Somchai's voice was soft, without bitterness. "She wanted 
children. A home. The ordinary things that ordinary people want. And I—I wanted 
something I couldn't name. Something that made me restless, that sent me 
wandering from city to city, from job to job, always searching for something I 
couldn't find." 
"Did you find it?" Prasert asked. 
"Eventually. But by then, she had married someone else. Had children. Built 
the life she wanted with a man who was willing to stay." 
"Do you regret it?" 
Somchai considered the question with the seriousness he gave to all questions. 
"I regret the pain I caused her. The confusion. The years she spent waiting for me 
to become something I wasn't. But the choice itself—" he shook his head slowly. 
"No. I don't regret that. We are what we are. Pretending otherwise only causes 
more suffering." 
"That sounds very philosophical," Anong said. "Also very lonely." 
"It was lonely. For a long time. But loneliness isn't the worst thing." Somchai 
smiled slightly. "The worst thing is being with someone and feeling lonely. At least 
when you're alone, you know where you stand." 
"That's surprisingly cynical for you," Prasert observed. 
"It's not cynical—it's honest. I've seen too many marriages where both people 
are miserable, staying together out of duty or fear or just habit. That's lonelier than 
any empty room." 
 
28 
"So you chose solitude over compromise." 
"I chose truth over comfort. Which doesn't make me better than either of 
you. Just different." 
❧ 
 
Anong was watching a young couple at a nearby table. They were in their 
twenties, probably, leaning toward each other, speaking in low voices, their hands 
intertwined on the table between them. They had that look—the look of people 
who believed their love was unique, unprecedented, a miracle the world had never 
seen before. 
"Look at them," Anong said. "Remember when we were like that?" 
"I was never like that," Prasert said. "Mei and I didn't hold hands in public. It 
wasn't done." 
"But you felt it. The excitement. The certainty. The feeling that nothing could 
possibly go wrong because love would protect you." 
"That's naivete, not love." 
"Is there a difference? When you're young, I mean?" 
Somchai watched the young couple with an expression of gentle melancholy. 
"They don't know what's coming," he said. "They think love is a destination. 
Somewhere you arrive and stay forever." 
"And it's not?" 
"Love is a verb. Something you do every day. Something you have to choose 
again and again, even when it's difficult. Especially when it's difficult." He took a 
sip of his tea. "That couple will face things they can't imagine right now. Illness. 
Financial troubles. The slow grinding wear of two different people trying to share 
 
29 
one life. And some of them—the lucky ones—will discover that love isn't what 
they thought it was. It's not a feeling. It's a practice." 
"You should write fortune cookies," Prasert said. 
"I considered it. The pay is terrible." 
The young couple laughed at something, their joy filling the tea house like 
sudden sunlight. Anong smiled at the sound. 
"I miss that," he said. "The laughing. Noi and I used to laugh all the time. 
Stupid things, private jokes that no one else would understand. I'd forgotten that, 
somehow. How much we laughed." 
"Laughter is the first thing to go," Somchai said. "When a relationship is 
struggling, the laughter disappears before anything else." 
"And when it comes back?" Anong asked. 
"Then you know you've survived something together." 
❧ 
 
The conversation drifted, as conversations do, from love to children, from 
children to regrets, from regrets back to love again. The rain had stopped entirely 
now, and steam was rising from the wet streets, giving the city a ghostly, ethereal 
quality. 
"My son doesn't speak to me," Prasert said abruptly. 
The other two waited. This, too, they had heard before, but the wound was 
still fresh even after all these years, and fresh wounds need air. 
"Five years now. He lives in America—California—with his wife and my 
grandchildren, whom I've never met. He sends a card at New Year. Sometimes a 
photo. But we don't talk." 
 
30 
"What happened?" Anong asked, though he knew the answer. 
"I happened. I pushed him too hard. Wanted him to be an engineer, like me. 
Wanted him to take over my projects, continue my work. And he—" Prasert's voice 
tightened. "He wanted to paint. To make art. Things I didn't understand and didn't 
try to understand." 
"And now?" 
"Now he's a successful artist in Los Angeles, and I'm an old man who will 
probably die without ever meeting his grandchildren." Prasert's hands were tight 
around his cup. "The worst part is, I know I was wrong. I've known for years. But 
I don't know how to say it. I don't know how to reach across that distance and tell 
him—" 
"Tell him what?" Somchai asked gently. 
"That I'm proud of him. That I was always proud of him. That I just didn't 
know how to show it." 
"Write him a letter," Anong suggested. "Say exactly that." 
"He wouldn't read it." 
"You don't know that." 
"I know my son." Prasert's voice was bitter. "He got his stubbornness from 
me. He'll hold onto that anger until one of us is dead. Probably until both of us are 
dead." 
"Pride is expensive," Somchai observed. "The most expensive thing there is. 
We pay for it with our relationships, our happiness, our peace of mind. And in the 
end, what does it buy us? An empty room and a clear conscience that keeps us 
awake at night." 
"I'm not sure my conscience is clear," Prasert admitted. 
"That's actually a good sign. If your conscience were clear, you wouldn't have 
learned anything." 
 
31 
❧ 
 
Anong had been thinking about his own children—four of them, scattered 
across the country, calling on holidays, visiting when they could, slowly building 
their own lives in which he played an increasingly minor role. 
"My daughter keeps trying to get me to move in with her," he said. "She has 
a big house in Chiang Mai. A garden. A room ready just for me, she says. Good 
air, clean water, no traffic." 
"Why don't you go?" Prasert asked. 
"Because I'm not ready to become someone's burden." Anong's voice was 
firm despite his age. "I've spent my whole life being useful. Building things. 
Providing for people. I'm not ready to become the person who needs to be 
provided for." 
"That's pride too," Somchai pointed out. 
"Maybe. But it's my pride, and I'm keeping it until I can't anymore." 
"When will that be?" 
Anong looked at his hands—hands that had hauled nets and counted money 
and held his wife as she took her last breath. "I don't know. When I can't get to 
this tea house on my own anymore, maybe. When I forget your names. When the 
burden of caring for me outweighs the gift of my company." He smiled sadly. "I 
hope I'll know when that moment comes. I hope I'll have the courage to stop 
fighting." 
"My mother didn't," Somchai said quietly. "She wanted to die at home, in her 
own bed, with her things around her. But the cancer was too aggressive. They took 
her to the hospital for pain management, and she never came back." 
"Did she know?" 
 
32 
"She knew. The last thing she said to me was: 'Don't let them keep you in a 
place like this.' And then—" Somchai's voice caught briefly. "Then she was gone." 
The three men sat in silence, each lost in their own memories of lost mothers, 
lost wives, lost pieces of themselves that would never come back. 
"We're a cheerful bunch," Prasert said finally. 
"We're an honest bunch," Anong corrected. "Which is worth more, at our 
age." 
❧ 
 
The young couple at the nearby table was getting up to leave, still holding 
hands, still glowing with that particular light of new love. As they passed, the young 
woman smiled at Anong—a warm, genuine smile from a stranger. 
"Thank you," she said, surprising everyone. 
"For what?" Anong asked. 
"For giving us hope. It's nice to see people who've been friends for so long. 
It makes us think maybe we'll make it too." 
Anong didn't know what to say. He watched them leave, hands still 
intertwined, stepping out into the wet street with the carelessness of youth and 
health. 
"We gave them hope," he said wonderingly. "Three old men complaining 
about their lives gave someone hope." 
"We're not complaining," Somchai said. "We're processing. There's a 
difference." 
"Is there?" 
 
33 
"Complaining is rejecting what is. Processing is accepting what is while 
learning from it." 
"I thought we were just drinking tea," Prasert muttered. 
"Tea is philosophy. Philosophy is tea. It's all the same river." 
"Your metaphors are getting worse with age." 
"Everything gets worse with age. That's the point." 
❧ 
 
As the afternoon wore on, the conversation turned to lighter things—gossip 
about neighbors, complaints about politics, debates about which tea house had the 
best dim sum (a question that could never be satisfactorily resolved because each 
man had his own irreplaceable memories tied to specific places). 
But underneath it all, the earlier talk of love still lingered, like the smell of rain 
on hot pavement. 
"Do you think it's possible to love someone completely?" Anong asked during 
a lull. "Without reservation? Without holding anything back?" 
Prasert considered this. "That sounds dangerous." 
"That sounds necessary," Somchai countered. "Half-hearted love is worse 
than no love at all. At least with no love, you know where you stand." 
"So you've said." 
"So I keep learning. The lesson doesn't stick." 
Anong was nodding slowly. "I think I loved Noi completely. Or at least, as 
completely as I was capable of. There was nothing I wouldn't have done for her. 
Nothing I wouldn't have given up." 
 
34 
"Would you have given up your pride?" Prasert asked, and his voice was 
carefully neutral. "Your sense of self? Your independence?" 
"I did. Gladly. That's what love asks of you. To let go of the things that keep 
you separate. To become part of something larger than yourself." 
"That sounds like drowning." 
"It felt like freedom." 
Somchai was quiet, listening to his friends with an expression that was hard 
to read. 
"Both things can be true," he said finally. "Love can be freedom and 
drowning. Salvation and destruction. The same fire that warms you can burn your 
house down." 
"So what's the answer?" Prasert demanded. "Love completely and risk 
destruction? Or hold back and risk nothing meaningful?" 
"The answer is different for everyone. For some people, the risk is worth it. 
For others, the preservation of self matters more. Neither is wrong." 
"That's a non-answer." 
"Most honest answers are." 
❧ 
 
The sun was beginning to set, painting the rain-washed city in shades of gold 
and pink. The streets had mostly dried, leaving only puddles that reflected the sky 
like scattered pieces of mirror. 
"Same time next week?" Anong asked as they began their slow ritual of 
departure. 
"Same time," Somchai confirmed. 
 
35 
"Assuming the world doesn't end before then," Prasert added, because 
pessimism was his love language. 
"The world has been ending since we were born," Anong said. "And yet here 
we are." 
"Here we are," Somchai agreed. "Still talking about love. Still trying to 
understand something that probably can't be understood." 
"Speak for yourself. I understand love perfectly." 
"Really, Prasert? Enlighten us." 
Prasert stood, steadying himself on his cane—a concession to age that he still 
resented even after ten years of using it. 
"Love is showing up," he said simply. "That's all. You show up when you 
don't want to. You show up when it's hard. You show up when you're angry, or 
tired, or convinced the other person is wrong about everything. And you keep 
showing up, day after day, until one of you can't anymore." 
He looked at his friends—these two men he had known for thirty years, with 
whom he had shared more of himself than he had shared with anyone, including 
his wife. 
"That's what we do," he said. "Every week. We show up." 
"That's the most romantic thing you've ever said," Anong said. 
"It wasn't meant to be romantic." 
"I know. That's what makes it romantic." 
They walked out together into the evening air, three old men who had loved 
and lost and somehow kept going. The city hummed around them, indifferent to 
their small dramas, their decades of history, their complicated hearts. 
But they didn't need the city to care. 
They had each other. 
 
36 
And for now, that was enough. 
❧ 
 
End of Chapter Two 
 
 
 
37 
 
 
 
Chapter Three 
The Illusion of Control 
In which plans are mocked and surrender is considered 
❧ 
 
The tea house was unusually empty when Somchai arrived—just a few 
scattered patrons and the familiar creak of old floorboards under his careful steps. 
He took his usual seat by the window and ordered his usual jasmine tea and waited, 
as he had waited every week for three decades. 
He was early. He was always early now. When you reach a certain age, 
punctuality becomes less a virtue and more a compulsion—a way of proving to 
yourself that you still have some say in when and where you exist in this world. 
Anong arrived next, ten minutes late and full of apologies. 
"The taxi driver took the wrong route," he said, settling into his chair with a 
series of small groans. "I told him to go down Silom, but he insisted on some 
shortcut that turned out to be anything but. We sat in traffic for twenty minutes, 
and I kept thinking: this is how I'm going to spend the last years of my life. In the 
back seat of a taxi, waiting for something to move." 
"You could have walked," Somchai observed. 
"I could have walked twenty years ago. Now I barely trust my knees to get me 
from the bathroom to the bedroom." 
 
38 
"Then you accept the taxi and its detours." 
"I accept nothing. I complain endlessly and change nothing. That's my 
spiritual practice." 
Somchai laughed—a warm, genuine sound that made Anong smile despite 
himself. 
"Where's Prasert?" Anong asked. "He's usually here before me, glaring at his 
watch and complaining about my tardiness." 
"Not here yet. Perhaps his taxi also took the scenic route." 
"Prasert doesn't take taxis. Prasert refuses to trust any driver who doesn't pass 
his personal engineering inspection. He takes the BTS like a man of principle." 
"The BTS breaks down too." 
"Yes, but when it does, Prasert can blame the system instead of the individual. 
That's much more satisfying for him." 
❧ 
 
They ordered tea and waited. The minutes stretched on—fifteen, twenty, 
twenty-five. Somchai felt the first cold fingers of worry beginning to touch his 
chest. 
"He's never this late," he said. 
"Traffic," Anong said, but his voice lacked conviction. 
"Prasert doesn't get stuck in traffic. Prasert accounts for traffic. He builds 
three redundancy systems into everything he does." 
"Maybe he overslept." 
"Prasert hasn't overslept since 1976." 
 
39 
Anong pulled out his phone—a concession to modernity that he still 
resented—and tried calling. The phone rang and rang, finally going to voicemail. 
"Prasert, it's Anong. Where are you? We're worried. Call back." 
He hung up and looked at Somchai with an expression that said everything 
neither of them wanted to say. 
"I'm sure he's fine," Somchai said, not believing it. 
"Of course he is. He's probably just—" 
The door opened, and Prasert walked in. 
Both men exhaled with relief that they would later deny feeling. 
But something was wrong. Prasert moved differently—slower, more careful, 
like a man navigating uncertain ground. He sat down heavily in his chair and stared 
at the table without speaking. 
"Are you all right?" Anong asked. 
Prasert was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was flat. 
"I fell. This morning. Getting out of bed. My leg just—gave out. Like it wasn't 
part of my body anymore." 
"Are you hurt?" 
"Bruised. Nothing serious. But—" he looked up, and for the first time his 
friends could see the fear in his eyes. "I couldn't get up. For fifteen minutes, I lay 
on the floor of my own bedroom, trying to get my body to do what I told it to do. 
And it wouldn't listen." 
Anong reached across and put his hand on Prasert's arm. The gesture was 
uncharacteristic, but the moment called for it. 
"Bodies fail," Somchai said gently. "It's what they do." 
 
40 
"I know that. I'm not an idiot. But knowing something and experiencing it—
" Prasert shook his head. "I've spent my entire life making things work. Buildings. 
Bridges. Systems. Everything I touched, I could fix or improve or at least 
understand. And now my own body is falling apart, and there's nothing I can do 
about it." 
"Welcome to being old," Anong said. "It's terrible. Would you like some tea?" 
Despite himself, Prasert almost smiled. "Is that your solution to everything?" 
"Tea and complaining. The two pillars of aged wisdom." 
❧ 
 
The waiter came and took orders—jasmine for Somchai, chrysanthemum for 
Prasert, plain hot water for Anong who had switched his usual preference, claiming 
his stomach couldn't handle flower tea anymore. 
"When did we become this?" Prasert asked. "A collection of symptoms and 
limitations? I used to run marathons. Did you know that? In my forties, I ran seven 
marathons. Now I can't walk down the stairs without holding the railing." 
"Pride," Somchai observed. 
"What?" 
"You're mourning your pride, not your legs. Your legs are doing what legs do 
when they've carried you for seventy-eight years. But your pride expected them to 
keep performing like they did at forty." 
"So I should just accept falling on my face?" 
"You should accept that falling is part of living. And getting up—or not 
getting up—is also part of living." 
"Easy for you to say. You're still walking around like a man half your age." 
 
41 
Somchai smiled. "I'm walking around like a man my exact age who has learned 
not to fight with his body. We have a truce, my body and I. I don't ask it to do 
things it can't do, and it doesn't humiliate me more than necessary." 
"That's a low bar." 
"It's the only bar that matters." 
❧ 
 
Anong had been quiet, stirring his hot water as if it were tea, lost in thoughts 
that played across his face like clouds. 
"Do you remember when we were young," he said finally, "how certain we 
were that we could control things?" 
Prasert snorted. "I'm still certain. I'm just bad at it now." 
"No, I mean really certain. I remember thinking—I was maybe thirty, maybe 
thirty-five—that if I worked hard enough and planned carefully enough, I could 
make anything happen. I could build my business, provide for my family, protect 
the people I loved from harm." 
"And?" Somchai asked. 
"And nothing. My business grew. I made money. I lost money. I made it back. 
My children grew up and became people I barely recognize. My wife got sick and 
died despite all my planning and all my money. None of my certainty protected 
anyone from anything." 
"That's not true," Somchai said. "Your certainty built a life. It gave your 
children stability. It gave your wife security." 
"But I couldn't give them what mattered most. I couldn't give them more 
time. I couldn't give them health. I couldn't give them happiness—not really, not 
the kind that lasts." 
 
42 
"No one can give those things. They're not ours to give." 
"Then what's the point?" Prasert demanded. "What's the point of working 
and planning and trying to control things if nothing is actually under our control?" 
Somchai leaned back in his chair and looked out the window at the street 
below, where traffic flowed and stopped and flowed again in patterns no one could 
fully predict or understand. 
"The point," he said slowly, "is not the outcome. It's the doing. We work not 
because we can guarantee results, but because working is what humans do. We plan 
not because plans succeed, but because planning helps us engage with life. We try 
to control things not because we can, but because the trying is how we participate 
in existence." 
"That sounds like a lot of effort for no payoff." 
"The effort is the payoff. The trying is the living. Results are just... weather. 
They come and go. They're not actually about us at all." 
❧ 
 
Prasert was looking at his hands—hands that had held blueprints and 
calculators and pens, that had shaped structures that still stood decades after he'd 
built them. Now those hands trembled slightly, and the skin was thin enough to 
see the veins beneath. 
"I built a bridge once," he said. "The Siriraj. You know it—everyone knows 
it. Three years of my life, maybe more. Every calculation, every inspection, every 
moment of doubt when I thought maybe we'd made a mistake somewhere. But we 
hadn't. The bridge stood. Still stands." 
"It's a beautiful bridge," Anong said. 
"It's a good bridge. Strong. Reliable. It'll probably outlast all of us." Prasert 
looked up. "But here's the thing I realized this morning, lying on my bedroom 
 
43 
floor: I was never in control of that bridge. Not really. I could do the math. I could 
check the welds. But earthquakes happen. Rivers flood. Materials fatigue in ways 
we can't predict. The bridge standing for forty years isn't because I controlled 
everything. It's because I was lucky." 
"Luck and skill," Somchai suggested. 
"Mostly luck. The skill just improved the odds slightly. But the universe could 
have knocked that bridge down any time it wanted. Still could. All my engineering, 
all my control—it's just a thin layer of paint over chaos." 
"You're only realizing this now?" Anong asked, and there was genuine 
surprise in his voice. 
"I always knew it intellectually. But this morning—" Prasert touched his leg, 
the one that had betrayed him. "This morning I felt it. The chaos lives in my own 
body. I can't control my own legs. How did I ever think I could control a river?" 
❧ 
 
The tea arrived, and they drank in silence for a while—not an uncomfortable 
silence, but the kind that comes when old friends have said something true and 
need time to absorb it. 
"I tried to control Noi's illness," Anong said eventually. "Flew in specialists 
from Singapore. Tried every treatment, every alternative therapy, every 
experimental drug I could find. Spent more money than I should tell my children 
about." 
"Did any of it help?" 
"Some of it extended her life. A few months here, a few months there. But in 
the end—" he shrugged, a small gesture that contained enormous grief. "In the 
end, she died anyway. And I had to ask myself: were those extra months worth the 
suffering they cost? The treatments were brutal. The hope was worse. Every time 
something seemed to work, we'd let ourselves believe, and then—" 
 
44 
"You couldn't control it," Somchai said. "But you couldn't not try." 
"Exactly. That's the cruelty of it. We know we can't control the outcome, but 
we're built to try anyway. It's like a curse. A beautiful, terrible curse." 
"Or maybe it's a gift," Somchai suggested. "The trying itself is meaningful, 
even when it fails. Especially when it fails." 
"Easy to say. Harder to feel." 
"Most wisdom is." 
❧ 
 
Prasert was still brooding over his fall—Anong could see it in the set of his 
jaw, the way his eyes kept drifting to his leg as if it might betray him again at any 
moment. 
"Tell me something," Prasert said. "Both of you. Honestly. How do you live 
with it? The knowing that nothing is actually under your control? How do you get 
up in the morning?" 
"Slowly," Anong said. "Very slowly. And with significant complaining." 
"I'm serious." 
Somchai set down his tea cup and thought carefully before answering. 
"I'll tell you a story," he said. "When I was young—maybe thirty, teaching at 
a small school in Chiang Rai—I had a student. A boy, maybe twelve years old. 
Brilliant mind. The kind of student who makes you remember why you became a 
teacher." 
"What happened to him?" 
"He got sick. Leukemia. Very aggressive. I visited him in the hospital every 
week, bringing books, trying to keep his mind active. And one day he asked me—
 
45 
this twelve-year-old boy with tubes coming out of his arms—he asked me why this 
was happening to him." 
"What did you say?" 
"I told him the truth. I said I didn't know. I said sometimes terrible things 
happen for no reason at all, and there's nothing we can do except face them as 
bravely as we can." 
"That seems harsh," Anong said. "For a child." 
"He appreciated the honesty. Children usually do. And then he said something 
I've never forgotten. He said: 'Maybe we're not supposed to control things. Maybe 
we're just supposed to experience them.'" 
Prasert was frowning. "That sounds like giving up." 
"That's what I thought too. At first. But the more I've lived, the more I think 
he was right. We spend so much energy trying to control outcomes—make things 
go the way we want, avoid the things we fear. But what if that's not our job? What 
if our job is just to be present for whatever happens? To experience fully, without 
resistance?" 
"That boy died," Prasert said. It wasn't a question. 
"Six months later. Peacefully, surrounded by family. And in those last months, 
he wasn't fighting anymore. He was just... living. Fully present, fully engaged, fully 
himself. More alive than most people I've ever known." 
❧ 
 
The afternoon was wearing on, and the tea house was beginning to fill with 
the pre-dinner crowd—office workers stopping for a quick drink, young couples 
on early dates, old people with nowhere else to be. 
 
46 
"I don't know if I can do that," Prasert admitted. "Just... let go. Accept. It feels 
like defeat." 
"Perhaps defeat is the wrong word," Somchai said. "Think of it as 
cooperation. Your body isn't your enemy—it's your partner. It's carried you for 
seventy-eight years. Maybe it's time to start listening to what it needs instead of 
demanding what you want." 
"My body needs to not fall over when I get out of bed." 
"Your body needs rest. Care. Gentleness. It needs you to stop treating it like 
a machine that can be pushed indefinitely." 
"I've never been gentle with anything in my life." 
"Then perhaps it's time to learn." 
Anong was nodding. "I've been thinking about this a lot, actually. Ever since 
Noi died. She was always the gentle one. She could accept things I couldn't. Rain, 
traffic, illness—whatever came, she would just... adjust. Bend instead of break." 
"And you?" 
"I break. Then I glue myself back together and pretend nothing happened." 
Anong smiled ruefully. "Not very wise, but it's gotten me this far." 
"Maybe there's another way," Somchai suggested. "Not breaking, not 
bending, but flowing. Like water around a rock." 
"I'm too old to become water," Prasert said. 
"You're too old not to try." 
❧ 
 
 
47 
The conversation shifted then, as it always did, to other topics—news from 
the neighborhood, complaints about rising prices, the endless low-level drama of 
existence that gave their meetings structure and meaning. 
But underneath it all, Prasert kept returning to that morning. The fall. The 
floor. The fifteen minutes that felt like fifteen hours. 
"I called my son," he said suddenly, interrupting Anong's story about a 
problematic plumber. 
Both men went quiet. Prasert never talked about his son—not since the 
estrangement, not since California had swallowed him up along with any hope of 
reconciliation. 
"What did he say?" Anong asked carefully. 
"Nothing. He didn't answer. I left a voicemail. Probably the most difficult 
voicemail I've ever left." 
"What did you say?" 
Prasert was quiet for a long moment. "I said I was sorry. That I'd been wrong. 
That I understood if he never wanted to speak to me again, but I needed him to 
know—" his voice cracked slightly. "I needed him to know that I loved him. That 
I'd always loved him. That I'd just been too stupid and too proud to say it." 
"That's good," Somchai said. "That's important." 
"Is it? He probably deleted it without listening." 
"Maybe. But you said it. That's what matters. You let go of the outcome and 
did what was right anyway." 
"I let go because I was lying on my bedroom floor, thinking I might be dying, 
and realizing I couldn't die with those words still stuck in my throat." 
"That's still letting go. The reason doesn't diminish the act." 
 
48 
Prasert shook his head slowly. "All those years of trying to control 
everything—my work, my family, my son's future. And it took falling on my face 
to realize I never controlled any of it. Not really. I was just making noise while the 
universe did whatever it wanted." 
"The noise mattered though," Anong said. "Your buildings still stand. Your 
bridges still carry people. Your son became an artist, which is what he wanted, 
partly because he was strong enough to resist you." 
"That's a strange way to take credit." 
"Take it anyway. We need all the credit we can get at our age." 
❧ 
 
The sun was beginning to set, painting the tea house windows in shades of 
amber and gold. Outside, the city was transforming from the harsh efficiency of 
day into the softer chaos of evening. 
"I should go," Prasert said, but he made no move to rise. 
"Your leg?" Anong asked. 
"My leg. My pride. My general reluctance to face another night alone in an 
apartment that echoes." 
"You could move," Somchai suggested. "Somewhere smaller. Somewhere 
with more people around." 
"And give up control of my own space? My own schedule? My own life?" 
"Give up the illusion of control. Keep the reality of living." 
Prasert almost laughed. "Is that supposed to be comforting?" 
"It's supposed to be true. Comfort and truth rarely coincide." 
 
49 
When they finally left—slowly, carefully, with much leaning on canes and 
arms—the evening was cool and quiet. The street seemed to hold its breath, waiting 
for them to pass. 
"Same time next week?" Anong asked, as always. 
"If my body cooperates," Prasert said. 
"Your body will do what it does. The question is whether you'll show up 
anyway." 
"I always show up." 
"Then I'll see you next week." 
They parted at the corner, each heading into their own particular loneliness, 
their own private negotiations with a universe that refused to be controlled. 
But something had shifted. Prasert could feel it in the way he walked—not 
slower exactly, but more careful. More attentive. As if, for the first time in seventy-
eight years, he was actually paying attention to the ground beneath his feet. 
It wasn't control. 
It was something better. 
It was presence. 
❧ 
 
End of Chapter Three 
 
 
 
50 
 
 
 
Chapter Four 
Joy and Suffering 
In which they argue about whether happiness requires sadness 
❧ 
 
Anong arrived at the tea house laughing. 
This was unusual enough that both Somchai and Prasert looked up in 
surprise. Anong was generally cheerful, yes, but his cheer tended toward the rueful 
end of the spectrum—the smile of a man who had seen too much to take anything 
entirely seriously. 
But today he was actually laughing—the kind of full-bodied, helpless laughter 
that shook his shoulders and made his eyes water. 
"What happened?" Prasert demanded, suspicious as always of unearned 
happiness. 
"I was—" Anong had to stop to catch his breath. "I was on my way here, and 
I passed a little girl trying to walk her cat on a leash." 
"A cat," Somchai said. 
"A cat. Huge orange thing. And it was just—" Anong started laughing again. 
"It was lying flat on the sidewalk, refusing to move, and this little girl was trying to 
drag it along, and the cat just looked so... offended. So personally insulted by the 
whole situation." 
 
51 
"And this made you laugh?" Prasert asked. "A girl torturing a cat?" 
"The cat wasn't being tortured. The cat was winning. The girl eventually 
picked it up and carried it, which is what the cat wanted all along." Anong wiped 
his eyes. "I don't know why it struck me as so funny. Maybe I'm losing my mind." 
"You lost your mind years ago," Prasert said. "We just didn't mention it out 
of politeness." 
"Laughter is good," Somchai said, smiling. "We don't laugh enough anymore." 
"We laugh constantly," Prasert countered. "Usually at each other." 
"That's mockery. It's not the same." 
"In our case, mockery is love." 
❧ 
 
The tea arrived—jasmine, chrysanthemum, and hot water, as always—and the 
three men settled into the comfortable rhythm of their weekly gathering. 
"Do you know what I was thinking about this morning?" Anong said, his 
laughter finally subsiding. "I was thinking about happiness. About whether we're 
happy." 
"We're old," Prasert said. "Happiness isn't the point anymore." 
"Then what is the point?" 
Prasert considered this. "Survival. Comfort. Not dying in embarrassing ways." 
"Those are conditions, not a point." 
"At our age, conditions are the point." 
 
52 
Somchai was listening with his usual attentive silence—the silence that meant 
he was organizing his thoughts into something that would probably be annoyingly 
profound. 
"I think," he said finally, "that we confuse happiness with comfort. We think 
if we can just be comfortable enough—no pain, no worry, no difficulty—then 
happiness will follow. But comfort and happiness aren't the same thing." 
"They're close enough," Prasert said. 
"Are they? Some of the happiest moments of my life were also the most 
uncomfortable. Climbing mountains when I was young. Teaching in villages with 
no electricity or running water. Those times were difficult, sometimes painful, but 
I was alive in a way that comfort never achieves." 
"You were young. Young people enjoy suffering because they don't know any 
better." 
"Maybe. Or maybe they know something we've forgotten." 
❧ 
 
Anong was nodding slowly. "My grandfather used to say that joy and suffering 
were the same thing—two sides of one coin. He said you couldn't have one without 
the other." 
"That sounds like rationalized pessimism," Prasert said. 
"It sounded like wisdom to me. And the older I get, the more I think he was 
right." Anong picked up his cup of hot water and held it, feeling the warmth seep 
into his arthritic fingers. "When Noi was sick—the worst time of my life—there 
were still moments of joy. Not despite the suffering, but somehow woven into it." 
"What kind of joy could you possibly find in that?" 
 
53 
"The joy of being with her. Of holding her hand while she slept. Of watching 
her smile at our grandchildren when they visited." Anong's voice was soft with 
memory. "I remember one day—she was too weak to get out of bed, and I was 
changing her sheets, and she said, 'Thank you for loving me enough to do this.' 
And in that moment—surrounded by pill bottles and medical equipment and the 
smell of sickness—I felt more loved than I ever had in my life." 
"That's beautiful," Somchai said. 
"It's sad," Prasert said. 
"Both things can be true," Anong replied. "That's what I'm trying to say. We 
think we have to choose—either happy or sad, either joy or suffering. But life 
doesn't work that way. Life gives us everything mixed together, and the skill is 
learning to taste all of it." 
❧ 
 
A young mother with a toddler sat down at a nearby table. The child was 
whining, not quite crying but clearly unhappy about something—the eternal, 
incomprehensible unhappiness of toddlers everywhere. 
Prasert watched them with an expression that might have been remembrance. 
"My son used to cry like that," he said. "When he was small. He'd cry over 
nothing—the wrong color cup, a slightly bruised banana, the arbitrary injustice of 
having to wear shoes. I used to get so frustrated." 
"All children cry over nothing," Anong said. "It's their job." 
"But I wanted him to be tough. I thought if I showed him that crying didn't 
get him what he wanted, he'd learn to manage his emotions. Be strong." 
"Did it work?" 
 
54 
"It worked perfectly." Prasert's voice was bitter. "He learned to hide his 
emotions so well that by the time he was a teenager, I had no idea what he was 
feeling. Ever. He became exactly what I asked for—a closed door I couldn't open." 
"Children are good at giving us what we ask for," Somchai observed. "The 
problem is we often ask for the wrong things." 
"Now you tell me." 
The toddler at the nearby table had stopped whining. His mother had 
produced some kind of toy, and he was now absorbed in it completely, his earlier 
distress forgotten as absolutely as if it had never existed. 
"Look at that," Anong said. "Complete despair to complete contentment in 
thirty seconds. If only we could do that." 
"We can't because we have memory," Somchai said. "The gift and curse of 
being adult is that we remember our suffering. We can't just let it go the way 
children do." 
"Is that really a curse?" Prasert asked. "Seems to me that remembering 
suffering is what keeps us from repeating mistakes." 
"Yes. But it also keeps us from experiencing joy fully. We're always waiting 
for the suffering to come back. Even when things are good, part of us is braced 
for them to become bad." 
"That's just being realistic." 
"Is it realistic? Or is it a kind of preemptive suffering? Suffering in advance 
for things that might never happen?" 
❧ 
 
 
55 
Somchai leaned back in his chair and looked out the window at the familiar 
street below. It was a beautiful day—clear sky, warm sun, the kind of weather that 
made even Bangkok seem gentle. 
"I want to tell you something," he said. "Something I've never told anyone." 
The other two waited. Somchai's rare personal revelations were always worth 
the wait. 
"When I was fifty-three," he continued, "I went through a period of—I don't 
know what to call it. Depression seems too clinical. Despair, perhaps. A deep, 
lasting despair that colored everything gray." 
"I didn't know that," Anong said. 
"No one did. I hid it well. I still taught my classes, still saw my friends, still 
went through all the motions of living. But inside, there was nothing. Just 
emptiness. The kind that makes you wonder why you bother getting out of bed." 
"What caused it?" Prasert asked. 
"I don't know. There was no specific event. One day I woke up and the 
meaning had drained out of everything. Food tasted like cardboard. Music was just 
noise. Even the beauty of the world—" Somchai gestured at the window, the sky, 
the light. "Even that seemed like a cruel joke. Here are all these beautiful things, 
and you can't feel any of them." 
"How long did it last?" 
"Nearly two years." 
"Two years," Anong breathed. "How did you survive?" 
"By waiting. That's all. I knew—intellectually, not emotionally—that feelings 
change. That the despair wouldn't last forever, even if it felt like it would. So I kept 
going through the motions, trusting that eventually the motions would become real 
again." 
"And they did?" 
 
56 
Somchai nodded slowly. "One morning I woke up and the gray was a little 
lighter. A few days later, lighter still. It took months to fully lift, and even now, 
decades later, I can feel it sometimes—just a shadow at the edge of things. But it 
taught me something I've never forgotten." 
"What?" 
"That joy and suffering are both temporary. Neither lasts. The suffering feels 
eternal when you're in it, and so does the joy. But both are passing states, like 
weather. The skill isn't in trying to hold onto joy or escape suffering. It's in learning 
to let both of them move through you without resistance." 
❧ 
 
Prasert was frowning, processing this information in his engineer's way—
trying to fit it into a framework that made sense. 
"So you're saying happiness is pointless?" he said finally. "Because it's going 
to end anyway?" 
"I'm saying the opposite. Happiness is precious precisely because it ends. If 
joy were permanent, it would become meaningless. It's the impermanence that 
gives it value." 
"That's very philosophical." 
"That's very true. Look at this moment, right now. We're here, together, alive, 
relatively comfortable. How many more moments like this will we have? A 
hundred? A thousand? It seems like a lot until you realize how quickly they pass." 
Anong was looking at his friends with an expression that was hard to read—
something between gratitude and grief. 
"I almost didn't come today," he admitted. "I woke up feeling... heavy. Missing 
Noi. Thinking about how much longer I can keep doing this. Getting out of bed. 
Coming to this tea house. Pretending everything is fine." 
 
57 
"You don't have to pretend," Somchai said gently. "Not with us." 
"I know. But sometimes the not-pretending feels worse. Like if I admit how 
hard it is, I'll fall apart completely." 
Prasert reached across the table and put his hand on Anong's arm—
awkwardly, because physical affection did not come naturally to him, but 
deliberately, because this was his friend and his friend was hurting. 
"We're here," Prasert said. "For whatever that's worth." 
"It's worth everything," Anong said. "That's what I was trying to explain. This 
morning was suffering. Real suffering, the kind that makes you want to stay in bed 
forever. But this—" he looked at their hands, the table, the tea, the window with 
its familiar light. "This is joy. And they're happening right next to each other. Not 
separately. Together." 
❧ 
 
The toddler at the nearby table had started whining again—a cyclical misery 
that seemed to have no beginning or end. His mother looked exhausted, that 
particular exhaustion of young parents who can't remember what sleep or silence 
feel like. 
"Should we help?" Anong asked. 
"With what?" Prasert said. "We're three old men. What do we know about 
children?" 
"We've all raised children." 
"And we all did it wrong. Different wrong, but still wrong." 
Despite himself, Somchai smiled. "He's not entirely incorrect." 
"I'm never entirely incorrect. I'm occasionally partially correct, which is the 
best any of us can hope for." 
 
58 
The mother caught Anong's eye and gave a rueful shrug—the universal 
gesture of parental resignation. Anong smiled back, wanting to tell her that it got 
easier, that the children grew up and the exhaustion faded and eventually you'd 
miss even this. 
But he didn't. Because it wasn't entirely true. The children grew up and 
became other kinds of difficult. The exhaustion changed forms but never really 
disappeared. And you didn't miss this—you missed the version of this that existed 
only in memory, smoothed over and made golden by time. 
"Every stage of life has its suffering," Somchai said, as if reading his thoughts. 
"And every stage has its joy. The mistake is thinking that some other stage would 
be better. That if we could only get to the next phase, or go back to the previous 
one, happiness would finally be available." 
"The grass is always greener," Prasert said. 
"The grass is always grass. We're the ones who keep painting it different 
colors." 
❧ 
 
The afternoon was deepening, and the tea house had filled with its usual mix 
of characters—the retired businessmen comparing portfolios, the young couples 
ignoring each other for their phones, the elderly women gossiping over small cups 
of expensive tea. 
"I had a dream last night," Anong said. "About Noi. She was standing in our 
kitchen, making breakfast, and I was sitting at the table watching her. And I 
knew—in the dream, I knew—that she was dead. That this was just a memory or 
a vision or whatever dreams are. But I also felt completely happy. More happy than 
I've felt since she died." 
"Because she was there," Somchai said. 
 
59 
"Because she was there, and I was there, and for one moment—one precious, 
impossible moment—we were together again." Anong's voice caught. "I woke up 
crying. But the strange thing is, I didn't feel sad. I felt... grateful. Like the universe 
had given me a gift I didn't deserve." 
"Why wouldn't you deserve it?" Prasert asked. 
"Because I had forty-eight years with her. Forty-eight years of waking up next 
to her, eating breakfast with her, arguing with her about stupid things and making 
up afterward. So many years. And other people—people who love just as much as 
I did—they get a few months. A few years if they're lucky. Why should I get even 
one more moment when I've already had more than my share?" 
"It doesn't work that way," Somchai said. "Love isn't a limited resource that 
runs out if you use too much. The universe doesn't keep track of who deserves 
what." 
"Then how does it work?" 
"I don't know. I don't think anyone knows. But I know that the joy of that 
dream was real. The grief of waking up was real. And both of them are part of 
loving her." 
❧ 
 
Prasert had been quiet, listening with the intensity he usually reserved for 
structural problems. 
"I don't dream about Mei," he said finally. "My wife. I haven't dreamed about 
her once since she died. Fifteen years, and not one dream." 
"That doesn't mean anything," Anong said. 
"Doesn't it? Fifteen years, and my subconscious has completely forgotten her? 
What does that say about our marriage?" 
 
60 
"It says your subconscious is a terrible measure of anything," Somchai said. 
"I dream about people I met once, thirty years ago, whose names I can't remember. 
I've never once dreamed about my mother. That doesn't mean I loved her less." 
"Maybe not. But sometimes I wonder—" Prasert stopped, started again. 
"Sometimes I wonder if I ever really loved her at all. Or if I just performed love 
well enough that neither of us noticed the difference." 
"There is no difference," Anong said firmly. "Love performed is still love. 
The intention doesn't matter as much as the action." 
"That's very pragmatic of you." 
"I'm a pragmatist. Always have been. I don't care why people are kind to me—
I just care that they're kind." 
"But don't you want to be loved for who you actually are?" Prasert asked. 
"Not for who you're pretending to be?" 
"There's no one I actually am," Anong said. "Not really. I'm different with 
different people. The me who talked to Noi isn't the same me who talks to you. 
The me who ran a business isn't the same me who plays with grandchildren. We're 
all made up of masks. The trick is finding people who love the masks." 
"That's depressing." 
"That's liberating. It means you don't have to find your 'true self' to be worthy 
of love. You just have to show up as whoever you are in that moment, and hope 
someone shows up back." 
❧ 
 
The sun was beginning its descent, painting the tea house in shades of amber 
that made everything look soft and old and precious. 
 
61 
"I think we're too hard on ourselves," Somchai said. "About happiness. About 
love. About all of it. We hold ourselves to standards that no one could meet—
perfect joy, perfect love, perfect understanding—and then we feel like failures 
when we fall short." 
"Aren't we failures?" Prasert asked, without self-pity—just genuine curiosity. 
"At what? At being perfect? Certainly. At being human? No. We've done 
exactly what humans do. We've loved imperfectly. Suffered imperfectly. Found joy 
in unexpected places and lost it in expected ones. That's not failure. That's life." 
"It feels like failure sometimes." 
"Feelings aren't facts. You taught me that, Prasert. Years ago, when I was 
going through my despair. You said: 'Your feelings are lying to you. The math 
doesn't support them.'" 
Prasert smiled slightly. "That sounds like something I'd say." 
"It helped. More than you know. Feelings lie to us all the time—especially 
feelings about our own worth. The skill is learning to question them without 
dismissing them entirely." 
"A fine line." 
"Most wisdom is." 
❧ 
 
As they prepared to leave—the slow gathering of belongings, the careful rising 
from chairs, the negotiation of who would pay this week—Anong turned to his 
friends with a look of unusual seriousness. 
"Can I ask you both something?" 
"Of course." 
 
62 
"Are you happy? Right now, today, in this moment—are you happy?" 
The question hung in the air, heavier than it should have been. 
Prasert spoke first. "I don't know. I'm not unhappy. Is that the same thing?" 
"Maybe. Somchai?" 
Somchai considered the question with his usual care. "In this moment, yes. 
I'm with friends. The tea is good. The light is beautiful. I'm aware that I'm alive, 
which is more than I can say for most moments." 
"And beyond this moment?" 
"Beyond this moment is beyond my concern. The future will contain 
whatever it contains. I'm trying to learn to meet it without anticipation." 
"That sounds enlightened." 
"It sounds like something I'm working on. The truth is, I'm terrified of what's 
coming. The body failing. The mind fading. The long slide toward an end I can't 
predict or control. But I'm trying not to let that fear steal the joy that's available 
now." 
Anong nodded slowly. "That's what I'm trying to do too. And mostly failing. 
But trying." 
"The trying is the practice," Somchai said. "The failing is the learning. There 
is no final achievement of happiness. There's only the daily attempt to be present 
for whatever shows up." 
❧ 
 
They walked out into the evening together, three old men who had argued 
about happiness without reaching any conclusions—which was, perhaps, the only 
honest way to discuss the subject. 
 
63 
The city hummed around them, indifferent and beautiful and continuing on 
as it always had, as it always would, long after they were gone. 
"Same time next week?" Anong asked. 
"If we're still here," Prasert said. 
"We'll be here," Somchai said. "One way or another." 
They parted at the corner, each carrying their own particular mixture of joy 
and suffering, their own private attempts to make sense of a world that refused to 
be sensible. 
And somewhere in the city, a little girl was still trying to walk her cat on a 
leash. 
And the cat was still winning. 
And life, despite everything, continued. 
❧ 
 
End of Chapter Four 
 
 
 
64 
 
 
 
Chapter Five 
Regret and Forgiveness 
In which old wounds are examined and some are finally allowed to heal 
❧ 
 
There was a new crack in the ceiling of the tea house—or perhaps it had 
always been there, and Somchai was only now noticing it. The line ran from the 
corner near the window to somewhere above the counter, a thin dark thread in the 
old plaster that spoke of settling, of age, of the slow inevitable process by which 
all things return to the earth. 
"You're staring at the ceiling," Prasert observed. 
"I'm watching it change," Somchai replied. "Though the change is too slow 
to actually see." 
"That's not watching. That's imagining." 
"Is there a difference?" 
Prasert opened his mouth to argue, then closed it. After thirty years of 
friendship, he recognized the conversations that led nowhere productive. 
Anong arrived late, slightly breathless, carrying a cardboard box that seemed 
to contain something precious—or at least something he was treating as precious, 
holding it carefully in both hands as he navigated between the tables. 
 
65 
"What's that?" Prasert asked, suspicious as always of unannounced objects. 
"My past," Anong said, setting the box on the table between them. "Or at 
least some of it. I was cleaning out the storage room, and I found this." 
He opened the box to reveal a jumble of photographs—old ones, the kind 
that were printed on thick paper and had wavy edges and colors that had faded to 
shades of orange and brown. 
"Family photos?" Somchai asked. 
"Everything photos. My whole life, apparently, thrown into a box and 
forgotten for decades." Anong began spreading the photographs on the table, 
arranging them with the absent-minded care of a man reacquainting himself with 
ghosts. 
"Here's Noi and me at our wedding—look how young we were. And this is 
my father, standing in front of the original fishing boat. And this—" he paused, 
picking up a photograph with a complicated expression. "This is my brother." 
The other two waited. Anong rarely mentioned his brother. 
"Sakorn. Three years older than me. We haven't spoken in thirty-seven years." 
"What happened?" Somchai asked, though he had heard pieces of this story 
before, scattered across decades of friendship like shards of a broken vase. 
Anong held the photograph closer to his face, as if trying to read something 
in his brother's young features. "The business happened. Our father died, left the 
fishing company to both of us equally. Sakorn wanted to modernize—new boats, 
new equipment, loans that made me nervous. I wanted to stay small, stay careful, 
stay safe." 
"And?" 
"And we fought. For two years we fought about everything—money, strategy, 
each other's character. It got ugly. Things were said that couldn't be unsaid. 
Eventually, he took his half and started his own company, and we never spoke 
again." 
 
66 
"Does he still live in Bangkok?" Prasert asked. 
"Chiang Mai. Or at least, that's where he was the last I heard. He could be 
dead for all I know. I could be mourning a man who's already been mourned." 
The photograph sat on the table between them—two young men, arms 
around each other's shoulders, grinning at the camera with the easy confidence of 
people who couldn't imagine ever being enemies. 
❧ 
 
"Why did you bring this?" Somchai asked. "The box, the photos. Why today?" 
Anong was quiet for a long moment, arranging and rearranging the 
photographs in patterns that seemed to have meaning only to him. 
"Because I'm old," he said finally. "Because I might not have many years left 
to decide what to do about this. Because I woke up this morning thinking about 
regrets, and Sakorn is the biggest regret of my life." 
"Bigger than not being there when your father died?" 
"That wasn't my fault—I was on a boat, three days from shore. But Sakorn—
" Anong shook his head. "Sakorn I did myself. I chose anger over love. I chose 
being right over being brothers. And I've had thirty-seven years to regret it." 
"Have you thought about reaching out?" Somchai asked gently. "Making 
contact?" 
"Every day. Every single day, I think about picking up the phone. And every 
day, I don't." 
"Why not?" 
"Because I'm afraid." Anong's voice was barely above a whisper. "I'm afraid 
he'll hang up. I'm afraid he'll tell me it's too late. I'm afraid he'll say the things I've 
 
67 
been saying to myself all these years—that I'm weak, that I'm a coward, that I 
destroyed our family for nothing." 
"And if he doesn't say those things?" 
"Then I've wasted thirty-seven years being afraid for nothing. Which might 
be worse." 
❧ 
 
Prasert had been listening with the uncomfortable expression of a man 
confronted with emotions he didn't know how to process. But something in 
Anong's confession had loosened something in him. 
"My father never forgave me," he said suddenly. 
The other two looked at him. 
"For what?" Anong asked. 
"For becoming an engineer instead of taking over his hardware store. For 
moving to Bangkok instead of staying in the village. For being—" Prasert made a 
gesture that encompassed himself, his life, all his choices. "For being who I am 
instead of who he wanted me to be." 
"He must have been proud eventually," Somchai said. "When you built the 
bridges. The buildings." 
"He never said so. He died when I was forty-two, and the last time we spoke, 
he called me an ungrateful son who had abandoned his family for foreign ideas." 
Prasert's voice was flat, the way it got when he was working hard to control it. "I 
didn't go to his funeral. I was working on a project in Cambodia, and I told myself 
I couldn't leave. But the truth is, I didn't want to face the village. Didn't want to 
see the accusation in everyone's eyes." 
"That's a heavy thing to carry," Somchai said. 
 
68 
"Thirty-six years. Slightly shorter than Anong's record." Prasert smiled, but 
there was no humor in it. "I tell myself it doesn't matter anymore. He's dead. His 
opinions are dust. But sometimes I wake up in the middle of the night, and I can 
hear him saying: 'This is what you left me for? This is what was so important?'" 
"What would you say to him?" Anong asked. "If you could speak to him 
now?" 
Prasert was quiet for a long time. The tea house noise swirled around them—
other conversations, the clink of cups, the distant sound of traffic—but their table 
had become an island of stillness. 
"I would say I'm sorry," Prasert said finally. "Not for my choices. I still think 
I was right to leave. But for the way I left. For the years of silence. For not being 
there when he was sick, when he was dying, when he needed his son and his son 
was too proud to come." 
"You were afraid too," Somchai observed. "Of confrontation. Of judgment. 
Of finding out that his love was conditional." 
"His love was conditional. That's not a fear—that's a fact." 
"Is it? Or is that what you told yourself to justify the distance?" 
Prasert didn't answer. 
❧ 
 
The afternoon light was shifting, and Somchai noticed that the crack in the 
ceiling caught the sun in a way that made it almost beautiful—a thread of gold in 
the worn plaster. 
"I have regrets too," he said, surprising himself. He hadn't planned to share 
this. 
 
69 
"You?" Prasert seemed genuinely startled. "You're the most accepting person 
I know. You practically invented letting go." 
"I practice letting go because I know how tight my grip used to be." Somchai 
picked up his tea cup, set it down again. "There was a student I failed. Years ago, 
when I was still teaching. A young man with problems I didn't understand—we 
didn't have words for it then. Depression, perhaps. Anxiety. Something that made 
him fragile in ways his classmates weren't." 
"What happened?" 
"He struggled. Failed exams. Stopped coming to class. I tried to reach him—
or I told myself I tried. Looking back, I think I was going through the motions. I 
was tired. I had thirty other students who also needed help. I couldn't save 
everyone." 
"That's reasonable," Anong said. 
"Reasonable, yes. Right, perhaps not." Somchai's voice was soft. "He took his 
own life at the end of the semester. Jumped from a building on campus. He was 
twenty years old." 
The silence that followed was heavy with things that couldn't be said. 
"That wasn't your fault," Prasert said finally. 
"I know that. Intellectually. But regret isn't about logic. I keep thinking: what 
if I had pushed harder? What if I had noticed the warning signs? What if I had told 
him that his struggles didn't define him, that there was help available, that life was 
worth continuing?" 
"You couldn't have known," Anong said. 
"Maybe not. But I should have tried harder to know. That's my regret. Not 
that I failed to save him—I probably couldn't have. But that I didn't try hard 
enough to see him." 
❧ 
 
70 
 
The three old men sat with their regrets spread between them like the 
photographs on the table—images of past failures, frozen in time, impossible to 
change but impossible to forget. 
"Is forgiveness possible?" Anong asked. "Real forgiveness, I mean. Not just 
saying the words."

The Last Conversation

He stared at the empty chairs and let himself remember. 
He remembered the day they met—three young men, barely thirty, with their 
whole lives ahead of them. They had been introduced by a mutual acquaintance at 
some forgettable event, and somehow the conversation had never stopped. Thirty 
years of conversations, piling up like leaves in autumn. 
He remembered Anong's laugh—that infectious, room-filling sound that 
made everyone around him smile whether they knew the joke or not. 
 
92 
He remembered Somchai's patient silences—the way he would wait until 
everyone else had spoken before offering his own thoughts, always careful, always 
kind. 
He remembered the way they had grown old together, their bodies failing, 
their minds sometimes wandering, but their friendship remaining solid, certain, the 
one thing in life he had never questioned. 
And he remembered the grief. 
The grief that had hit him like a wall when Anong died. The grief that had 
crushed him when Somchai followed. The grief so large and so total that his mind 
had simply refused to accept it—had constructed an elaborate fiction to avoid 
facing the unbearable truth. 
He was alone. 
He had been alone for three years. 
He would be alone until he died. 
❧ 
 
The sun was setting. The light through the window had turned that familiar 
gold, the gold of thirty years of afternoons, and Prasert knew what he had to do. 
He had to say goodbye. 
"Somchai," he said softly, looking at the empty chair. "Anong. I know you're 
not there. I know you've been gone for years. But I need to say this, even if no 
one's listening." 
The tea house hummed around him, oblivious to his grief, absorbed in its 
own small dramas. 
"You were the best parts of my life. Both of you. I spent so many years 
pretending I didn't need anyone, pretending I was complete by myself, and then 
 
93 
you came along and proved me wrong. You showed me what friendship could be. 
What love could be. What it meant to show up, week after week, for no reason 
except that you wanted to be together." 
His voice cracked, but he kept going. 
"I'm sorry I couldn't let you go. I'm sorry I made you stay—in my head, in 
my heart, in these conversations that must have seemed so crazy to everyone 
watching. But I wasn't ready. I wasn't ready to face a world without you in it." 
He picked up the cup of jasmine tea—Somchai's tea—and held it, feeling the 
warmth seep into his aged hands. 
"Somchai, you taught me that wisdom isn't about having answers. It's about 
being brave enough to keep asking questions. You showed me that surrender isn't 
weakness—it's the only real strength there is." 
He set down the jasmine tea and picked up the hot water—Anong's drink, 
because Anong had always been the practical one. 
"Anong, you taught me that love isn't something you feel. It's something you 
do. Every day. Even when it's hard. Especially when it's hard. You showed me 
what it looked like to show up for the people you care about, no matter what." 
He set down the hot water and looked at his own cup—the chrysanthemum 
tea he had been drinking for thirty years. 
"I don't know how to do this without you. I don't know who I am when I'm 
not the skeptic arguing with the sage and the storyteller. But I have to learn. 
Because you're gone, and I'm still here, and I have to figure out how to be alive in 
a world that doesn't have you in it." 
❧ 
 
Prasert stayed at the table until the tea house closed. 
 
94 
Mrs. Chen came by several times, bringing more tea, taking away the 
untouched cups, asking if he was all right. He nodded each time, not trusting 
himself to speak. 
When finally he rose to leave, his joints aching, his heart emptier than it had 
ever been, he paused at the door and looked back at the table by the window. 
For just a moment, he could have sworn he saw them there—Somchai with 
his gentle smile, Anong with his hands gesturing wildly as he told some story—and 
his heart seized with a grief so vast it threatened to swallow him whole. 
But they weren't there. 
They would never be there again. 
And he had to learn to live with that. 
❧ 
 
The street was dark and quiet when Prasert stepped outside. The city that had 
seemed so familiar for so many decades now felt foreign, hostile, a place where he 
no longer belonged. 
He walked slowly, using his cane, heading toward an apartment that had been 
empty for three years—empty of love, empty of friendship, empty of everything 
except an old man and his regrets. 
He was halfway home when he stopped. 
There was a bench by the canal—a simple wooden bench where old men 
sometimes sat in the afternoons, watching the boats go by, talking about nothing, 
passing the time until the time ran out. 
Prasert sat down heavily. 
 
95 
The water of the canal reflected the city lights, turning the surface into a 
rippling mirror of gold and silver. A boat passed by, its engine chugging softly in 
the darkness. 
"I'm tired," Prasert said aloud. To no one. To himself. To the night. 
He was tired. Tired of pretending. Tired of remembering. Tired of being the 
last one left, the survivor who had outlived the things worth surviving for. 
He thought about the letter he had sent to his son. It would arrive in California 
soon. Maybe his son would read it. Maybe his son would respond. Maybe, after all 
these years, there was still a chance for reconciliation. 
But sitting here in the darkness, Prasert wasn't sure he cared anymore. 
He had spent so much of his life building things—bridges, buildings, systems 
that worked, structures that would outlast him. But what was the point of lasting? 
His bridges would stand for another hundred years, and he would be forgotten. 
His buildings would house people who had never heard his name, never knew the 
man who had calculated every stress, checked every joint, made sure they would 
be safe. 
And his friends—his friends were gone, and the only monument to their 
friendship was an old man on a bench, crying in the darkness. 
❧ 
 
He didn't know how long he sat there. 
The night grew cooler, and the city sounds softened, and the boats stopped 
passing on the canal. The city was sleeping, and Prasert sat in the darkness, letting 
the grief finally, finally have its way. 
He cried for Anong, for the stories that would never be told, the laughter that 
would never fill a room again. 
 
96 
He cried for Somchai, for the wisdom that was lost, the gentle presence that 
could make any pain bearable. 
He cried for himself, for the proud, foolish man who had spent three years 
talking to ghosts rather than face the unbearable truth. 
And somewhere in the crying, something broke. 
Not his heart—that had been broken for years. 
Something else. 
The wall he had built against the grief. The fiction he had maintained. The 
desperate belief that if he just kept going through the motions, if he just kept 
pretending, he could outrun the loss. 
It broke, and the grief poured through, and Prasert let it come. 
❧ 
 
When the first light of dawn touched the eastern sky, Prasert was still on the 
bench. 
He was cold. His joints ached. His face was swollen from crying, and his chest 
felt like it had been emptied of everything that had ever filled it. 
But he was still here. 
Still breathing. 
Still alive, against all odds and despite all evidence that there was nothing left 
to be alive for. 
He stood slowly, using the bench for support, feeling every one of his seventy-
eight years in his bones. 
 
97 
The city was waking up around him. He could hear the first motorcycles 
starting, the first vendors setting up their carts, the first signs of life returning to a 
world that had kept spinning through the night. 
He walked toward home. 
Not because he wanted to. Not because anything waited for him there. 
But because that's what you did. 
You kept going. 
Even when going meant nothing. 
Even when the people you loved were gone. 
Even when your heart was broken and your mind was shattered and your 
whole world had collapsed into grief. 
You kept going. 
Because Somchai would have told him to. 
Because Anong would have laughed at him for giving up. 
Because somewhere, in a version of himself he couldn't quite remember, 
Prasert had been a man who didn't give up. A man who faced problems and solved 
them. A man who built bridges across impossible rivers. 
He could build a bridge across this. 
Maybe. 
Probably not. 
But he would try. 
❧ 
 
 
98 
Three weeks later, a letter arrived from California. 
Prasert held it in his trembling hands for a long time before opening it. Inside, 
in his son's careful handwriting, were the words he had stopped hoping to hear. 
Dad, 
I read your letter. I read it seven times. I cried. 
I forgive you. I think I forgave you years ago, but I was too proud to say it. 
The grandchildren know your name. They've seen your bridges. They ask about you. 
Come to California. Please. We want to meet you. 
Before it's too late. 
Your son, 
Chai 
❧ 
 
Prasert flew to Los Angeles on a Tuesday in March. 
His son met him at the airport—older than Prasert remembered, gray in his 
hair, wrinkles around his eyes. But the same face. The same stubborn jaw. The 
same way of standing, slightly awkward, not quite sure what to do with his hands. 
"Dad," Chai said. 
"Son." 
They stood there for a long moment, two men with thirty years of silence 
between them, not knowing how to begin. 
Then Chai stepped forward and hugged him. 
 
99 
It was awkward. It was clumsy. It was nothing like the graceful reconciliations 
in movies. 
It was perfect. 
❧ 
 
Prasert spent two weeks in California. 
He met his grandchildren—three of them, ages eight to fourteen, shy at first 
but warming up quickly when they discovered their grandfather knew things about 
buildings and bridges that their teachers didn't. 
He walked with his son through the California streets, talking about art and 
engineering, discovering that these things weren't as different as he'd once believed. 
He told them about his friends. About Anong and his stories. About Somchai 
and his wisdom. About thirty years of afternoons at a tea house in Bangkok. 
He told them about the three years of conversations with people who weren't 
there. 
And his son said: "That's not crazy, Dad. That's love. That's what love does 
when it can't let go." 
❧ 
 
On his last night in California, Prasert dreamed of the tea house. 
He was sitting at the table by the window, and Somchai was there, and Anong 
was there, and the light was golden, and everything was exactly as it had been for 
thirty years. 
"You came back," Anong said, grinning. "I knew you would." 
 
100 
"I never left," Prasert said. "Not really." 
"No," Somchai agreed. "You never did." 
They sat together in the dream, talking about nothing, laughing at old jokes, 
being together the way they had always been together. And Prasert knew it wasn't 
real—knew it was just his mind giving him one last gift—but he didn't care. 
Real or not, they were here. 
Real or not, he loved them. 
Real or not, some things never end. 
❧ 
 
He woke up crying. 
But this time, the tears weren't just grief. 
They were gratitude. 
For thirty years of friendship. 
For the privilege of having people worth missing. 
For the gift of love, even when love meant loss. 
Even when loss was unbearable. 
Even when unbearable was the only thing left. 
❧ 
 
Prasert returned to Bangkok a different man. 
 
101 
He went back to the tea house. Sat at the table by the window. Ordered one 
cup of chrysanthemum tea. 
Just one. 
Mrs. Chen brought it to him with a smile that understood everything. 
"Welcome back, Mr. Prasert," she said. 
"Thank you," he replied. 
He sat there alone, drinking his tea, watching the light fall through the window 
in long golden stripes. 
And somewhere inside him, in the part of his heart where his friends still 
lived, he could hear them laughing. 
❧ 
Three months later, Prasert died in his sleep. 
They found him in his apartment, in his bed, with a smile on his face and a 
photograph clutched in his hand. 
The photograph showed three young men, arms around each other's 
shoulders, grinning at the camera in front of a tea house that was still there, still 
standing, still waiting for the next generation of friends to claim their table by the 
window. 
At his funeral, his son spoke about bridges. 
About connections. 
About the way some structures outlast the people who build them, carrying 
others across rivers that would otherwise be impossible to cross. 
"My father built bridges his whole life," Chai said. "But the most important 
bridge he built was to his friends. A bridge that held for thirty years. A bridge that 
holds still, even now. Even after." 
 
102 
In the back of the temple, an old woman wiped her eyes—Mrs. Chen, who 
had come to pay her respects to the last of the three old men. 
She thought about the table by the window. 
She thought about jasmine tea and chrysanthemum tea and plain hot water. 
She thought about the way three voices had filled her tea house for thirty 
years, and then two, and then one, and now none. 
And she decided that the table would stay reserved. 
Forever. 
For the three old men who had taught her what friendship looked like. 
What love looked like. 
What it meant to keep showing up, week after week, year after year, until you 
couldn't anymore. 
And even after that. 
Even after. 
❧

Afterword 
 
 
❧ 
 
This book began as a conversation. 
Not a grand philosophical discussion, not a literary salon, but a simple 
conversation between old friends at a tea house in Bangkok—one I happened to 
overhear while waiting for my own tea on a rainy afternoon many years ago. 
I don't remember what they were talking about. Something about time, 
perhaps. Or loss. The specifics faded long ago. But what stayed with me was the 
quality of their attention—the way these three old men listened to each other with 
such care, such presence, such obvious love. 
I watched them for maybe an hour, pretending to read a newspaper while 
really studying the architecture of their friendship. The way one would speak and 
the others would lean in. The way they laughed—not politely, but genuinely, their 
whole bodies shaking. The way silences fell between them without awkwardness, 
filled instead with the comfortable weight of decades. 
When they left, I felt a profound sense of loss—absurd, since I didn't know 
them, had never spoken to them, would never see them again. But I had witnessed 
something rare: human beings being fully human with each other, without 
performance, without pretense. 
I started writing this book that night. 
❧ 
 
105 
 
The characters in these pages are fictional, though they carry pieces of many 
real people—grandparents, teachers, friends, strangers on trains who told me more 
in a single conversation than years of formal education. 
Somchai is built from every wise person I've been lucky enough to know—
people who had learned to hold the world lightly, who could laugh at their own 
contradictions, who understood that certainty was the enemy of wisdom. 
Prasert is built from every skeptic I've loved—people who demanded 
evidence, who refused easy answers, who nonetheless surprised me with the depth 
of their hidden hearts. 
Anong is built from every storyteller—the ones who understood that facts 
matter less than truth, that memory is a creative act, that the best way to say 
something important is to say something entertaining first. 
Together, they became something more than their parts—a friendship I 
found myself missing before I'd finished writing it. 
❧ 
 
Some readers may notice certain philosophical threads running through these 
conversations—ideas about impermanence, about attachment, about the 
possibility of finding peace in the midst of loss. 
I have not named these ideas or their sources. This was deliberate. The best 
wisdom is the kind that sneaks up on you, that arrives dressed as ordinary 
conversation, that you don't recognize until it has already changed you. 
If something in these pages made you think, made you feel, made you want 
to call an old friend—then the wisdom has done its work. Where it came from 
matters less than where it leads you. 
❧

The tea house is real. 
I won't tell you its name or address. You probably couldn't find it anyway—
Bangkok changes too quickly, and places like this tend to disappear when you're 
not looking. 
But there are tea houses like it in every city. Places where old friends gather. 
Places where the conversations that really matter happen. 
Find yours, if you can. 
Show up every week. 
Talk about time and love and death and all the things that make us human. 
And when you lose the people you love—which you will, because that's how 
love works—keep showing up anyway. 
Keep the conversation going. 
They're still listening.

Somchai, known as "the wise one," spent his life as a teacher in schools 
across Thailand. He never married, never had children, but considered every

something you practiced—imperfectly, daily, with humility and humor. He died at 
seventy-six, of a stroke, surrounded by the books he loved and the memories of all 
the lives he had touched. 
Anong, known as "the storyteller," built a fishing business from nothing, 
raised four children, and loved his wife Noi for forty-eight years until her death. 
He believed that every person was a story waiting to be told, and he spent his life 
collecting stories the way others collect coins or stamps. He died at seventy-nine, 
his heart finally giving out after a lifetime of feeling everything too deeply. 
Prasert, known as "the skeptic," was an engineer who built bridges across 
rivers that had never been crossed. He loved his wife Mei in his own quiet way for 
forty-two years, and spent the last fifteen years of his life learning—slowly, 
reluctantly—to say the things he should have been saying all along. He died at 
seventy-eight, having finally made peace with the son he had driven away and the 
friends he couldn't let go of. 
They met every week for thirty years at a tea house in Bangkok. 
They are gone now, all three of them. 
But the conversation continues. 
It always does.
