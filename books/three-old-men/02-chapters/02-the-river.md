# Chapter Two
## The River

The following Thursday was warmer than expected—one of those October gifts when the summer decides it isn't quite finished, sends back one last postcard of warmth before disappearing entirely. Bernard had worn his heavy coat and was now sweating in it, too stubborn to admit he'd overdressed.

"We could walk," Yusuf suggested. "Instead of sitting."

"Walk where?"

"Does it matter?"

"Of course it matters. You can't just walk aimlessly. You need a destination."

Teo, who had walked aimlessly for most of his life—through cities and careers and relationships, meandering toward no particular goal—said, "The river. We haven't been to the river in years."

Bernard considered this. The river was a fifteen-minute walk from the park, through the old industrial district that was slowly becoming condominiums and coffee shops. It wasn't a great river—nothing like the Hudson or the Seine—just a modest waterway that had once powered mills and was now mostly decorative, lined with walking paths and benches where young couples sat and pretended to watch the water while actually watching each other.

"Fine," Bernard said. "The river."

Yusuf was already standing, already moving toward the park's eastern exit as though he had known all along where they were going. Bernard sometimes wondered if Yusuf could see the future, or if he was simply so comfortable with uncertainty that he flowed toward whatever came next without resistance. It was infuriating. It was also, Bernard had to admit, somewhat enviable.

They walked slowly—three old men navigating sidewalks designed for faster feet. Teo had his cane today, the one with the silver handle shaped like a treble clef. He didn't always need it, but he liked the drama of it, the way it announced his presence. Bernard walked with his hands in his pockets, counting his steps out of habit, measuring the distance without meaning to. Yusuf walked like he was already where he was going.

"The old mill is gone," Teo observed as they passed a construction site. "They're building apartments."

"Everything is apartments now," Bernard said. "This whole city is becoming one giant apartment building."

"You live in an apartment."

"I live in a brownstone. It's different."

"How is it different?"

"It has character. History. It was built by craftsmen who cared about what they were making. These new buildings—" Bernard gestured at the construction site, at the exposed steel beams and concrete forms— "they're designed by algorithms. Optimized for profit, not people."

"And yet people will live in them," Yusuf said. "They will fall in love in them, raise children in them, grow old in them. The building doesn't know it was designed by an algorithm. The people inside will make it a home regardless."

Bernard wanted to argue but couldn't find the thread. Yusuf had a habit of doing that—acknowledging a complaint and then gently dissolving it, leaving Bernard holding air instead of grievance.

The construction site gave way to a stretch of renovated warehouses, now home to art galleries and restaurants with single-word names. A young woman in a vintage dress walked past, eyes on her phone, and didn't look up when Teo tipped his imaginary hat. Twenty years ago, he would have tried to catch her eye, maybe attempted some charming comment about the weather or her earrings or the cosmic significance of their paths crossing. Now he just watched her go, a faint smile on his face.

"Do you miss it?" he asked, to no one in particular.

"Miss what?" Bernard asked.

"Being young. Being noticed. Being... in the world, instead of watching it go past."

"I'm still in the world."

"You know what I mean."

Bernard did know. He just didn't want to admit it. The slow slide into invisibility was one of the cruelest tricks of age—the gradual realization that the world was moving on without you, that your opinions and experiences and hard-won wisdom were becoming irrelevant, that soon enough you would be a ghost even before you were gone.

"I don't miss being young," Yusuf said. "Young people are in a hurry. They don't know yet that there's nowhere to rush to."

"That's easy to say when you're past the rushing," Bernard said.

"Is it easy? I don't think so. I think it's the hardest lesson there is. To stop rushing and just... be. Most people never learn it."

"You sound like a fortune cookie."

"Fortune cookies are often wise. That's why we break them open."

They reached the river a few minutes later—or rather, they reached the walkway that ran along it, a paved path bordered by benches and lampposts and the occasional sculpture that was probably meaningful to someone. The water was brown and slow, carrying leaves and sticks and the general detritus of urban life. Not beautiful, exactly, but present. Real. Moving steadily toward wherever rivers go when they leave the city.

Teo found a bench—this one with a clear view of the water—and lowered himself onto it with his usual production of sighs and groans. Bernard sat beside him, taking a moment to appreciate the absence of the heavy coat he'd finally removed and was now carrying over his arm like a punishment. Yusuf stood by the railing, looking out at the water.

"Heraclitus said you can't step into the same river twice," Teo offered.

"Heraclitus never tried to cross the Vistula in January," Bernard said. "That river freezes solid. You could step into it a hundred times if you wanted."

"That's not what he meant."

"I know what he meant. The river changes, we change, everything changes, nothing stays the same. It's not a particularly revolutionary insight."

"And yet we fight it," Yusuf said, still watching the water. "Every day, we fight it. We want things to stay as they were. We want the people we love to never change, the places we know to always be there. We want to step into the same river and find it just as we left it."

"Is that so wrong?" Teo asked.

"It's not wrong. It's just... impossible. And the gap between what we want and what is possible—that gap is where suffering lives."

Bernard shifted on the bench, uncomfortable with the direction of the conversation. He preferred concrete things, problems with solutions, questions with answers. This kind of talk—about suffering and impermanence and the nature of reality—made him feel unmoored, as though the ground beneath him were less solid than he'd assumed.

"I don't suffer because things change," he said. "I suffer because my knee hurts and my children don't call and the world seems determined to forget everything that used to matter."

"And those are not changes?" Yusuf turned from the railing to look at him. "Your knee used to not hurt. Your children used to call. The world used to remember the things you knew. The suffering comes from wanting the old version, the previous version, the self and the world that no longer exist."

"So I should just... accept it? Let everything go? Pretend I don't care?"

"Acceptance is not pretending. It's the opposite of pretending. It's seeing clearly what is, instead of wishing for what was or hoping for what might be."

Teo was nodding slowly, but Bernard could see that Yusuf's words were landing somewhere deep in him, hitting something tender. Teo had never been good at accepting change. He still thought about Elena, fifty years gone. He still played the same piece of music when he was sad, the Elgar concerto that Jerzy had performed the night everything felt possible. He still kept a photograph of his childhood home, even though the building had been torn down decades ago.

"I went back, you know," Teo said quietly. "To where I grew up. About ten years ago."

"I didn't know that," Bernard said.

"I didn't tell anyone. I just... went. I was in Warsaw for a funeral, and afterward, I took a taxi to my old neighborhood." He was looking at the river but not seeing it, his eyes focused on something much further away. "It wasn't there. The building, I mean. The whole block was gone. They'd put up a shopping center. Glass and steel and advertisements for things I'd never heard of. I stood on the sidewalk where the entrance used to be, and there was nothing. Not even a plaque. Not even a sign that it had ever been there."

Yusuf left the railing and came to sit beside them, on Bernard's other side. The bench was not quite wide enough for three, and they pressed together like schoolboys, shoulder to shoulder.

"What did you feel?" Yusuf asked.

"At first? Rage. Pure, clean rage. How dare they tear down my building? How dare they erase my memories? I wanted to find someone to blame, someone to punish. I wanted to stand in the middle of that shopping center and scream until someone understood what had been lost."

"But you didn't."

"No. I didn't." Teo smiled, but it was the kind of smile that held something broken. "Instead, I walked. I walked all day, through streets I used to know, and most of them were different too. The bakery where my mother bought bread—gone. The school where I learned to read music—converted into offices. The church where I was baptized—still there, thank God, but surrounded by new buildings that dwarfed it, made it look small and apologetic."

"And by the end of the day?" Bernard asked. He was surprised to find himself genuinely curious. Teo told a lot of stories, but this one felt different—rawer, more essential.

"By the end of the day, I realized something. The city I was mourning—the Warsaw of my childhood—didn't disappear because they built a shopping center. It disappeared the moment I left it. It disappeared the moment my mother died, and my father, and everyone else who remembered it the way I did. The buildings were just... symbols. Markers on a map that no longer existed."

"That's terribly sad," Bernard said.

"Is it? I used to think so. But now I wonder if it's not also... liberating? If the places we love exist mostly in our memory, then we carry them with us everywhere. I don't need the building. I have the building. It's here—" he tapped his chest— "and here—" he tapped his temple— "and it will stay as long as I do. Maybe longer, if I tell the stories to someone who listens."

Yusuf reached over and put his hand on Teo's shoulder. It was a simple gesture, almost nothing, but Teo's face changed when he felt it—softened, released something he'd been holding.

"The stories matter," Yusuf said. "They're how we keep each other. I don't mean alive—nothing keeps us alive forever. But present. Visible. The stories are how we say: this person was here, and they mattered, and I won't let them vanish entirely."

"Even when we're telling the stories to ourselves?" Teo asked.

"Especially then."

---

They sat by the river for a long time, watching the water move. A duck family paddled past—mother and four ducklings, ridiculously fluffy, paddling furiously to keep up. A jogger ran by, then another, then a woman walking a dog that seemed to be walking her. The clouds shifted and the light changed, and the river stayed the same by always being different.

Bernard, who had been silent for several minutes, suddenly said: "The Carpenter Street Bridge."

The others waited.

"My first major project. Just out of engineering school, still terrified I didn't know enough, and they gave me the Carpenter Street Bridge. It wasn't much—a pedestrian crossing over the railroad tracks—but it was mine. I designed every beam, calculated every load, spent six months of my life making sure it would stand for a hundred years."

"I remember that bridge," Teo said. "The one with the blue railings?"

"Yes. The blue railings were my idea. Everyone else wanted gray, but I argued for blue. Said it would make people happy to cross it. Said it would become a landmark." Bernard paused, his hands tightening on the coat in his lap. "They replaced it last year. The whole thing. Tore it down and put up some modern monstrosity with LED lights and electronic signs. Said the old one was 'structurally outdated.'"

"I'm sorry," Teo said.

"It's not about being sorry. It's about..." Bernard trailed off, searching for the word. "I put part of myself into that bridge. Not metaphorically—literally. There was a day, near the end of construction, when the foreman let me sign my name in the wet concrete of one of the support pillars. My name. My handwriting. Right there at the base of the bridge, where no one would ever see it but where it would be part of the structure forever."

"And now it's gone," Yusuf said. Not a question.

"Now it's gone. They jackhammered it to rubble and carted it away to some landfill. My name, my signature, my proof that I existed—buried under someone's garbage."

The bitterness in Bernard's voice was thick, almost visceral. Teo recognized it; he'd felt it himself, standing on the sidewalk in Warsaw. The particular grief of watching your proof of existence disappear.

"The bridge served for how many years?" Yusuf asked.

"Fifty-four."

"And in those fifty-four years, how many people crossed it?"

Bernard frowned, calculating despite himself. "I don't know. Thousands? Tens of thousands?"

"Hundreds of thousands, probably. People going to work, going home, going to see their lovers, going to say goodbye to their mothers. Children learning to walk, running across for the first time. Old people, like us, taking it slow, grateful for the blue railings that made them happy even if they didn't know why." Yusuf's voice was gentle, insistent. "The bridge lived, Bernard. It had a life. It touched other lives. And now it's done, and something new is there, and that thing will touch other lives in ways you can't imagine."

"But my name—"

"Your name was always going to disappear. Everything with a name disappears eventually. Ozymandias built an empire, and now there's nothing left but sand and a broken statue. But the people who walked across your bridge—they're still here. Some of them. And the memories they made while crossing, the conversations they had, the hands they held—those are still rippling outward, affecting things neither you nor I will ever know about."

Bernard was quiet for a long moment. When he finally spoke, his voice was different—smaller, younger, almost plaintive.

"I just wanted to leave something behind. Something that would prove I was here."

"You have," Yusuf said. "But not in the way you thought. Not in concrete and steel. In the way you helped the people cross. In the blue railings that made them smile. In your children, even if they don't call. In us. In this conversation. In this afternoon, by this river, which will never happen again and will therefore never be lost."

The river kept flowing. The ducks reappeared, heading the other direction now, the mother still leading, the ducklings still paddling furiously. The light was turning golden, the way it did in late October, when the sun hung low and everything looked like a memory of itself.

"I don't understand you, Yusuf," Bernard said finally.

"No?"

"No. You talk about loss and disappearance like they're gifts. Like we should be grateful that everything we love is going to crumble. I can't do that. I can't look at the world falling apart and feel... peaceful."

"I don't feel peaceful because the world is falling apart. I feel peaceful because it always has been, and yet here we are. Still sitting. Still talking. Still together." Yusuf spread his hands, a gesture that encompassed the river, the bench, the three of them. "This moment will end. This afternoon will end. Someday, we will end. But right now—right now—we are here. And that is enough. It has to be enough, because it's all we actually have."

Teo had been listening quietly, his eyes on the water. Now he said: "Do you really believe that? That this moment is all we have?"

"I know it," Yusuf said. "Not as a theory, but as a fact. Everything else—the past, the future, the bridges we've built, the buildings we've lost—those are stories. Useful stories, sometimes beautiful stories, but stories nonetheless. The only thing that is not a story is now. And now is always ending and always beginning."

"That sounds exhausting."

"It is, until you stop fighting it. Then it becomes... simple. Not easy—simple. There's a difference."

The evening was coming on, the way evenings do in autumn—quickly, with that particular urgency that says winter is on its way. Bernard stood, brushing off his trousers, reaching for the coat he'd been carrying.

"We should head back."

"Yes," Teo agreed, though he didn't move immediately. He was still watching the river, still thinking about the building in Warsaw, about the crossing he'd made ten thousand times as a child and would never make again.

Yusuf stood last, and again Bernard noticed that quality about him—the lightness, as though he were already somewhere else. As though being here were a choice he was actively making, moment by moment.

They walked back through the twilight, through the galleries and the condominiums and the construction site where the mill used to be. The air had cooled, and Bernard put on his coat, and Teo leaned a little more heavily on his cane, and Yusuf walked between them like something both solid and insubstantial, like a bridge between what was and what would be.

"Same time next week?" Teo asked, when they reached the intersection.

"Same time," Bernard said.

Yusuf just smiled.

"What?" Bernard asked.

"Nothing. I was just thinking... you asked about the same time, the same place. But they won't be the same. They'll be new. Different. A new river, as Heraclitus would say."

"Heraclitus never met us," Bernard said. "We're the same cranky old men every Thursday, no matter what the philosophers claim."

"Are you?" Yusuf asked. "The same?"

Bernard opened his mouth to answer, then closed it. Because the truth was—he didn't know. He felt like the same person he'd been last week, but how could he be sure? His cells were dying and dividing, his thoughts were shifting, his relationship to the world was constantly being renegotiated. Maybe the Bernard who walked away from the river was a different Bernard than the one who had arrived. Maybe the only thing that stayed constant was the name.

"I'll see you next week," he said finally, because it was easier than philosophy.

"I'll be here," Yusuf said.

And something about the way he said it—I'll be here—made Teo turn and look at him, made him want to reach out and touch his friend's arm just to confirm he was solid. But the moment passed, and Yusuf was already walking away, and the streetlights were coming on, and it was just another Thursday evening, ordinary and unremarkable, like all the others before it and all the ones to come.

Or so Teo told himself.

Later, much later, he would remember this evening and wonder.
