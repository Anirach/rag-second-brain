# Chapter Three
## The Illusion of Control

*In which plans are mocked and surrender is considered*

---

The tea house was unusually empty when Somchai arrived—just a few scattered patrons and the familiar creak of old floorboards under his careful steps. He took his usual seat by the window and ordered his usual jasmine tea and waited, as he had waited every week for three decades.

He was early. He was always early now. When you reach a certain age, punctuality becomes less a virtue and more a compulsion—a way of proving to yourself that you still have some say in when and where you exist in this world.

Anong arrived next, ten minutes late and full of apologies.

"The taxi driver took the wrong route," he said, settling into his chair with a series of small groans. "I told him to go down Silom, but he insisted on some shortcut that turned out to be anything but. We sat in traffic for twenty minutes, and I kept thinking: this is how I'm going to spend the last years of my life. In the back seat of a taxi, waiting for something to move."

"You could have walked," Somchai observed.

"I could have walked twenty years ago. Now I barely trust my knees to get me from the bathroom to the bedroom."

"Then you accept the taxi and its detours."

"I accept nothing. I complain endlessly and change nothing. That's my spiritual practice."

Somchai laughed—a warm, genuine sound that made Anong smile despite himself.

"Where's Prasert?" Anong asked. "He's usually here before me, glaring at his watch and complaining about my tardiness."

"Not here yet. Perhaps his taxi also took the scenic route."

"Prasert doesn't take taxis. Prasert refuses to trust any driver who doesn't pass his personal engineering inspection. He takes the BTS like a man of principle."

"The BTS breaks down too."

"Yes, but when it does, Prasert can blame the system instead of the individual. That's much more satisfying for him."

---

They ordered tea and waited. The minutes stretched on—fifteen, twenty, twenty-five. Somchai felt the first cold fingers of worry beginning to touch his chest.

"He's never this late," he said.

"Traffic," Anong said, but his voice lacked conviction.

"Prasert doesn't get stuck in traffic. Prasert accounts for traffic. He builds three redundancy systems into everything he does."

"Maybe he overslept."

"Prasert hasn't overslept since 1976."

Anong pulled out his phone—a concession to modernity that he still resented—and tried calling. The phone rang and rang, finally going to voicemail.

"Prasert, it's Anong. Where are you? We're worried. Call back."

He hung up and looked at Somchai with an expression that said everything neither of them wanted to say.

"I'm sure he's fine," Somchai said, not believing it.

"Of course he is. He's probably just—"

The door opened, and Prasert walked in.

Both men exhaled with relief that they would later deny feeling.

But something was wrong. Prasert moved differently—slower, more careful, like a man navigating uncertain ground. He sat down heavily in his chair and stared at the table without speaking.

"Are you all right?" Anong asked.

Prasert was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was flat.

"I fell. This morning. Getting out of bed. My leg just—gave out. Like it wasn't part of my body anymore."

"Are you hurt?"

"Bruised. Nothing serious. But—" he looked up, and for the first time his friends could see the fear in his eyes. "I couldn't get up. For fifteen minutes, I lay on the floor of my own bedroom, trying to get my body to do what I told it to do. And it wouldn't listen."

Anong reached across and put his hand on Prasert's arm. The gesture was uncharacteristic, but the moment called for it.

"Bodies fail," Somchai said gently. "It's what they do."

"I know that. I'm not an idiot. But knowing something and experiencing it—" Prasert shook his head. "I've spent my entire life making things work. Buildings. Bridges. Systems. Everything I touched, I could fix or improve or at least understand. And now my own body is falling apart, and there's nothing I can do about it."

"Welcome to being old," Anong said. "It's terrible. Would you like some tea?"

Despite himself, Prasert almost smiled. "Is that your solution to everything?"

"Tea and complaining. The two pillars of aged wisdom."

---

The waiter came and took orders—jasmine for Somchai, chrysanthemum for Prasert, plain hot water for Anong who had switched his usual preference, claiming his stomach couldn't handle flower tea anymore.

"When did we become this?" Prasert asked. "A collection of symptoms and limitations? I used to run marathons. Did you know that? In my forties, I ran seven marathons. Now I can't walk down the stairs without holding the railing."

"Pride," Somchai observed.

"What?"

"You're mourning your pride, not your legs. Your legs are doing what legs do when they've carried you for seventy-eight years. But your pride expected them to keep performing like they did at forty."

"So I should just accept falling on my face?"

"You should accept that falling is part of living. And getting up—or not getting up—is also part of living."

"Easy for you to say. You're still walking around like a man half your age."

Somchai smiled. "I'm walking around like a man my exact age who has learned not to fight with his body. We have a truce, my body and I. I don't ask it to do things it can't do, and it doesn't humiliate me more than necessary."

"That's a low bar."

"It's the only bar that matters."

---

Anong had been quiet, stirring his hot water as if it were tea, lost in thoughts that played across his face like clouds.

"Do you remember when we were young," he said finally, "how certain we were that we could control things?"

Prasert snorted. "I'm still certain. I'm just bad at it now."

"No, I mean really certain. I remember thinking—I was maybe thirty, maybe thirty-five—that if I worked hard enough and planned carefully enough, I could make anything happen. I could build my business, provide for my family, protect the people I loved from harm."

"And?" Somchai asked.

"And nothing. My business grew. I made money. I lost money. I made it back. My children grew up and became people I barely recognize. My wife got sick and died despite all my planning and all my money. None of my certainty protected anyone from anything."

"That's not true," Somchai said. "Your certainty built a life. It gave your children stability. It gave your wife security."

"But I couldn't give them what mattered most. I couldn't give them more time. I couldn't give them health. I couldn't give them happiness—not really, not the kind that lasts."

"No one can give those things. They're not ours to give."

"Then what's the point?" Prasert demanded. "What's the point of working and planning and trying to control things if nothing is actually under our control?"

Somchai leaned back in his chair and looked out the window at the street below, where traffic flowed and stopped and flowed again in patterns no one could fully predict or understand.

"The point," he said slowly, "is not the outcome. It's the doing. We work not because we can guarantee results, but because working is what humans do. We plan not because plans succeed, but because planning helps us engage with life. We try to control things not because we can, but because the trying is how we participate in existence."

"That sounds like a lot of effort for no payoff."

"The effort is the payoff. The trying is the living. Results are just... weather. They come and go. They're not actually about us at all."

---

Prasert was looking at his hands—hands that had held blueprints and calculators and pens, that had shaped structures that still stood decades after he'd built them. Now those hands trembled slightly, and the skin was thin enough to see the veins beneath.

"I built a bridge once," he said. "The Siriraj. You know it—everyone knows it. Three years of my life, maybe more. Every calculation, every inspection, every moment of doubt when I thought maybe we'd made a mistake somewhere. But we hadn't. The bridge stood. Still stands."

"It's a beautiful bridge," Anong said.

"It's a good bridge. Strong. Reliable. It'll probably outlast all of us." Prasert looked up. "But here's the thing I realized this morning, lying on my bedroom floor: I was never in control of that bridge. Not really. I could do the math. I could check the welds. But earthquakes happen. Rivers flood. Materials fatigue in ways we can't predict. The bridge standing for forty years isn't because I controlled everything. It's because I was lucky."

"Luck and skill," Somchai suggested.

"Mostly luck. The skill just improved the odds slightly. But the universe could have knocked that bridge down any time it wanted. Still could. All my engineering, all my control—it's just a thin layer of paint over chaos."

"You're only realizing this now?" Anong asked, and there was genuine surprise in his voice.

"I always knew it intellectually. But this morning—" Prasert touched his leg, the one that had betrayed him. "This morning I felt it. The chaos lives in my own body. I can't control my own legs. How did I ever think I could control a river?"

---

The tea arrived, and they drank in silence for a while—not an uncomfortable silence, but the kind that comes when old friends have said something true and need time to absorb it.

"I tried to control Noi's illness," Anong said eventually. "Flew in specialists from Singapore. Tried every treatment, every alternative therapy, every experimental drug I could find. Spent more money than I should tell my children about."

"Did any of it help?"

"Some of it extended her life. A few months here, a few months there. But in the end—" he shrugged, a small gesture that contained enormous grief. "In the end, she died anyway. And I had to ask myself: were those extra months worth the suffering they cost? The treatments were brutal. The hope was worse. Every time something seemed to work, we'd let ourselves believe, and then—"

"You couldn't control it," Somchai said. "But you couldn't not try."

"Exactly. That's the cruelty of it. We know we can't control the outcome, but we're built to try anyway. It's like a curse. A beautiful, terrible curse."

"Or maybe it's a gift," Somchai suggested. "The trying itself is meaningful, even when it fails. Especially when it fails."

"Easy to say. Harder to feel."

"Most wisdom is."

---

Prasert was still brooding over his fall—Anong could see it in the set of his jaw, the way his eyes kept drifting to his leg as if it might betray him again at any moment.

"Tell me something," Prasert said. "Both of you. Honestly. How do you live with it? The knowing that nothing is actually under your control? How do you get up in the morning?"

"Slowly," Anong said. "Very slowly. And with significant complaining."

"I'm serious."

Somchai set down his tea cup and thought carefully before answering.

"I'll tell you a story," he said. "When I was young—maybe thirty, teaching at a small school in Chiang Rai—I had a student. A boy, maybe twelve years old. Brilliant mind. The kind of student who makes you remember why you became a teacher."

"What happened to him?"

"He got sick. Leukemia. Very aggressive. I visited him in the hospital every week, bringing books, trying to keep his mind active. And one day he asked me—this twelve-year-old boy with tubes coming out of his arms—he asked me why this was happening to him."

"What did you say?"

"I told him the truth. I said I didn't know. I said sometimes terrible things happen for no reason at all, and there's nothing we can do except face them as bravely as we can."

"That seems harsh," Anong said. "For a child."

"He appreciated the honesty. Children usually do. And then he said something I've never forgotten. He said: 'Maybe we're not supposed to control things. Maybe we're just supposed to experience them.'"

Prasert was frowning. "That sounds like giving up."

"That's what I thought too. At first. But the more I've lived, the more I think he was right. We spend so much energy trying to control outcomes—make things go the way we want, avoid the things we fear. But what if that's not our job? What if our job is just to be present for whatever happens? To experience fully, without resistance?"

"That boy died," Prasert said. It wasn't a question.

"Six months later. Peacefully, surrounded by family. And in those last months, he wasn't fighting anymore. He was just... living. Fully present, fully engaged, fully himself. More alive than most people I've ever known."

---

The afternoon was wearing on, and the tea house was beginning to fill with the pre-dinner crowd—office workers stopping for a quick drink, young couples on early dates, old people with nowhere else to be.

"I don't know if I can do that," Prasert admitted. "Just... let go. Accept. It feels like defeat."

"Perhaps defeat is the wrong word," Somchai said. "Think of it as cooperation. Your body isn't your enemy—it's your partner. It's carried you for seventy-eight years. Maybe it's time to start listening to what it needs instead of demanding what you want."

"My body needs to not fall over when I get out of bed."

"Your body needs rest. Care. Gentleness. It needs you to stop treating it like a machine that can be pushed indefinitely."

"I've never been gentle with anything in my life."

"Then perhaps it's time to learn."

Anong was nodding. "I've been thinking about this a lot, actually. Ever since Noi died. She was always the gentle one. She could accept things I couldn't. Rain, traffic, illness—whatever came, she would just... adjust. Bend instead of break."

"And you?"

"I break. Then I glue myself back together and pretend nothing happened." Anong smiled ruefully. "Not very wise, but it's gotten me this far."

"Maybe there's another way," Somchai suggested. "Not breaking, not bending, but flowing. Like water around a rock."

"I'm too old to become water," Prasert said.

"You're too old not to try."

---

The conversation shifted then, as it always did, to other topics—news from the neighborhood, complaints about rising prices, the endless low-level drama of existence that gave their meetings structure and meaning.

But underneath it all, Prasert kept returning to that morning. The fall. The floor. The fifteen minutes that felt like fifteen hours.

"I called my son," he said suddenly, interrupting Anong's story about a problematic plumber.

Both men went quiet. Prasert never talked about his son—not since the estrangement, not since California had swallowed him up along with any hope of reconciliation.

"What did he say?" Anong asked carefully.

"Nothing. He didn't answer. I left a voicemail. Probably the most difficult voicemail I've ever left."

"What did you say?"

Prasert was quiet for a long moment. "I said I was sorry. That I'd been wrong. That I understood if he never wanted to speak to me again, but I needed him to know—" his voice cracked slightly. "I needed him to know that I loved him. That I'd always loved him. That I'd just been too stupid and too proud to say it."

"That's good," Somchai said. "That's important."

"Is it? He probably deleted it without listening."

"Maybe. But you said it. That's what matters. You let go of the outcome and did what was right anyway."

"I let go because I was lying on my bedroom floor, thinking I might be dying, and realizing I couldn't die with those words still stuck in my throat."

"That's still letting go. The reason doesn't diminish the act."

Prasert shook his head slowly. "All those years of trying to control everything—my work, my family, my son's future. And it took falling on my face to realize I never controlled any of it. Not really. I was just making noise while the universe did whatever it wanted."

"The noise mattered though," Anong said. "Your buildings still stand. Your bridges still carry people. Your son became an artist, which is what he wanted, partly because he was strong enough to resist you."

"That's a strange way to take credit."

"Take it anyway. We need all the credit we can get at our age."

---

The sun was beginning to set, painting the tea house windows in shades of amber and gold. Outside, the city was transforming from the harsh efficiency of day into the softer chaos of evening.

"I should go," Prasert said, but he made no move to rise.

"Your leg?" Anong asked.

"My leg. My pride. My general reluctance to face another night alone in an apartment that echoes."

"You could move," Somchai suggested. "Somewhere smaller. Somewhere with more people around."

"And give up control of my own space? My own schedule? My own life?"

"Give up the illusion of control. Keep the reality of living."

Prasert almost laughed. "Is that supposed to be comforting?"

"It's supposed to be true. Comfort and truth rarely coincide."

When they finally left—slowly, carefully, with much leaning on canes and arms—the evening was cool and quiet. The street seemed to hold its breath, waiting for them to pass.

"Same time next week?" Anong asked, as always.

"If my body cooperates," Prasert said.

"Your body will do what it does. The question is whether you'll show up anyway."

"I always show up."

"Then I'll see you next week."

They parted at the corner, each heading into their own particular loneliness, their own private negotiations with a universe that refused to be controlled.

But something had shifted. Prasert could feel it in the way he walked—not slower exactly, but more careful. More attentive. As if, for the first time in seventy-eight years, he was actually paying attention to the ground beneath his feet.

It wasn't control.

It was something better.

It was presence.

---

*End of Chapter Three*
