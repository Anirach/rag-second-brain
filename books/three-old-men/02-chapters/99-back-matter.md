# Afterword

## Author's Note

---

This book began as a conversation.

Not a grand philosophical discussion, not a literary salon, but a simple conversation between old friends at a tea house in Bangkok—one I happened to overhear while waiting for my own tea on a rainy afternoon many years ago.

I don't remember what they were talking about. Something about time, perhaps. Or loss. The specifics faded long ago. But what stayed with me was the quality of their attention—the way these three old men listened to each other with such care, such presence, such obvious love.

I watched them for maybe an hour, pretending to read a newspaper while really studying the architecture of their friendship. The way one would speak and the others would lean in. The way they laughed—not politely, but genuinely, their whole bodies shaking. The way silences fell between them without awkwardness, filled instead with the comfortable weight of decades.

When they left, I felt a profound sense of loss—absurd, since I didn't know them, had never spoken to them, would never see them again. But I had witnessed something rare: human beings being fully human with each other, without performance, without pretense.

I started writing this book that night.

---

The characters in these pages are fictional, though they carry pieces of many real people—grandparents, teachers, friends, strangers on trains who told me more in a single conversation than years of formal education.

Somchai is built from every wise person I've been lucky enough to know—people who had learned to hold the world lightly, who could laugh at their own contradictions, who understood that certainty was the enemy of wisdom.

Prasert is built from every skeptic I've loved—people who demanded evidence, who refused easy answers, who nonetheless surprised me with the depth of their hidden hearts.

Anong is built from every storyteller—the ones who understood that facts matter less than truth, that memory is a creative act, that the best way to say something important is to say something entertaining first.

Together, they became something more than their parts—a friendship I found myself missing before I'd finished writing it.

---

Some readers may notice certain philosophical threads running through these conversations—ideas about impermanence, about attachment, about the possibility of finding peace in the midst of loss.

I have not named these ideas or their sources. This was deliberate. The best wisdom is the kind that sneaks up on you, that arrives dressed as ordinary conversation, that you don't recognize until it has already changed you.

If something in these pages made you think, made you feel, made you want to call an old friend—then the wisdom has done its work. Where it came from matters less than where it leads you.

---

The tea house is real.

I won't tell you its name or address. You probably couldn't find it anyway—Bangkok changes too quickly, and places like this tend to disappear when you're not looking.

But there are tea houses like it in every city. Places where old friends gather. Places where the conversations that really matter happen.

Find yours, if you can.

Show up every week.

Talk about time and love and death and all the things that make us human.

And when you lose the people you love—which you will, because that's how love works—keep showing up anyway.

Keep the conversation going.

They're still listening.

---

*Bangkok, February 2026*

It would not exist without every tea house, coffee shop, and bar where people sit together and talk about real things. Thank you for existing. Thank you for staying open. Thank you for giving us places where we can be human with each other.

Don't wait.

We never have as much time as we think.

# About the Characters

**Somchai**, known as "the wise one," spent his life as a teacher in schools across Thailand. He never married, never had children, but considered every student his family. He believed that wisdom was not something you achieved but something you practiced—imperfectly, daily, with humility and humor. He died at seventy-six, of a stroke, surrounded by the books he loved and the memories of all the lives he had touched.

**Anong**, known as "the storyteller," built a fishing business from nothing, raised four children, and loved his wife Noi for forty-seven years until her death. He believed that every person was a story waiting to be told, and he spent his life collecting stories the way others collect coins or stamps. He died at seventy-nine, his heart finally giving out after a lifetime of feeling everything too deeply.

**Prasert**, known as "the skeptic," was an engineer who built bridges across rivers that had never been crossed. He loved his wife Mei in his own quiet way for forty-two years, and spent the last fifteen years of his life learning—slowly, reluctantly—to say the things he should have been saying all along. He died at seventy-eight, having finally made peace with the son he had driven away and the friends he couldn't let go of.

They met every week for thirty years at a tea house in Bangkok.

They are gone now, all three of them.

But the conversation continues.

It always does.

# A Final Note

If you have read this far, I have a request.

Think of someone you love—someone you've been meaning to call, or visit, or write to. Someone you've been putting off because life is busy and there's always tomorrow.

Contact them today.

Don't wait for the perfect moment. The perfect moment is now.

Say the things you've been meaning to say. Ask the questions you've been afraid to ask. Listen the way you would listen if you knew this was the last conversation you would ever have.

Because one day, it will be.

And when that day comes, you don't want to be left with the words still stuck in your throat.

You want to be left with the certainty that you loved well, and were loved in return, and that nothing important was left unsaid.

That's the only kind of ending worth having.
