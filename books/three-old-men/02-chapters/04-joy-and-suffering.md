# Chapter Four
## Joy and Suffering

*In which they argue about whether happiness requires sadness, and no one wins*

---

Anong arrived at the tea house laughing.

This was unusual enough that both Somchai and Prasert looked up in surprise. Anong was generally cheerful, yes, but his cheer tended toward the rueful end of the spectrum—the smile of a man who had seen too much to take anything entirely seriously.

But today he was actually laughing—the kind of full-bodied, helpless laughter that shook his shoulders and made his eyes water.

"What happened?" Prasert demanded, suspicious as always of unearned happiness.

"I was—" Anong had to stop to catch his breath. "I was on my way here, and I passed a little girl trying to walk her cat on a leash."

"A cat," Somchai said.

"A cat. Huge orange thing. And it was just—" Anong started laughing again. "It was lying flat on the sidewalk, refusing to move, and this little girl was trying to drag it along, and the cat just looked so... offended. So personally insulted by the whole situation."

"And this made you laugh?" Prasert asked. "A girl torturing a cat?"

"The cat wasn't being tortured. The cat was winning. The girl eventually picked it up and carried it, which is what the cat wanted all along." Anong wiped his eyes. "I don't know why it struck me as so funny. Maybe I'm losing my mind."

"You lost your mind years ago," Prasert said. "We just didn't mention it out of politeness."

"Laughter is good," Somchai said, smiling. "We don't laugh enough anymore."

"We laugh constantly," Prasert countered. "Usually at each other."

"That's mockery. It's not the same."

"In our case, mockery is love."

---

The tea arrived—jasmine, chrysanthemum, and hot water, as always—and the three men settled into the comfortable rhythm of their weekly gathering.

"Do you know what I was thinking about this morning?" Anong said, his laughter finally subsiding. "I was thinking about happiness. About whether we're happy."

"We're old," Prasert said. "Happiness isn't the point anymore."

"Then what is the point?"

Prasert considered this. "Survival. Comfort. Not dying in embarrassing ways."

"Those are conditions, not a point."

"At our age, conditions are the point."

Somchai was listening with his usual attentive silence—the silence that meant he was organizing his thoughts into something that would probably be annoyingly profound.

"I think," he said finally, "that we confuse happiness with comfort. We think if we can just be comfortable enough—no pain, no worry, no difficulty—then happiness will follow. But comfort and happiness aren't the same thing."

"They're close enough," Prasert said.

"Are they? Some of the happiest moments of my life were also the most uncomfortable. Climbing mountains when I was young. Teaching in villages with no electricity or running water. Those times were difficult, sometimes painful, but I was alive in a way that comfort never achieves."

"You were young. Young people enjoy suffering because they don't know any better."

"Maybe. Or maybe they know something we've forgotten."

---

Anong was nodding slowly. "My grandfather used to say that joy and suffering were the same thing—two sides of one coin. He said you couldn't have one without the other."

"That sounds like rationalized pessimism," Prasert said.

"It sounded like wisdom to me. And the older I get, the more I think he was right." Anong picked up his cup of hot water and held it, feeling the warmth seep into his arthritic fingers. "When Noi was sick—the worst time of my life—there were still moments of joy. Not despite the suffering, but somehow woven into it."

"What kind of joy could you possibly find in that?"

"The joy of being with her. Of holding her hand while she slept. Of watching her smile at our grandchildren when they visited." Anong's voice was soft with memory. "I remember one day—she was too weak to get out of bed, and I was changing her sheets, and she said, 'Thank you for loving me enough to do this.' And in that moment—surrounded by pill bottles and medical equipment and the smell of sickness—I felt more loved than I ever had in my life."

"That's beautiful," Somchai said.

"It's sad," Prasert said.

"Both things can be true," Anong replied. "That's what I'm trying to say. We think we have to choose—either happy or sad, either joy or suffering. But life doesn't work that way. Life gives us everything mixed together, and the skill is learning to taste all of it."

---

A young mother with a toddler sat down at a nearby table. The child was whining, not quite crying but clearly unhappy about something—the eternal, incomprehensible unhappiness of toddlers everywhere.

Prasert watched them with an expression that might have been remembrance.

"My son used to cry like that," he said. "When he was small. He'd cry over nothing—the wrong color cup, a slightly bruised banana, the arbitrary injustice of having to wear shoes. I used to get so frustrated."

"All children cry over nothing," Anong said. "It's their job."

"But I wanted him to be tough. I thought if I showed him that crying didn't get him what he wanted, he'd learn to manage his emotions. Be strong."

"Did it work?"

"It worked perfectly." Prasert's voice was bitter. "He learned to hide his emotions so well that by the time he was a teenager, I had no idea what he was feeling. Ever. He became exactly what I asked for—a closed door I couldn't open."

"Children are good at giving us what we ask for," Somchai observed. "The problem is we often ask for the wrong things."

"Now you tell me."

The toddler at the nearby table had stopped whining. His mother had produced some kind of toy, and he was now absorbed in it completely, his earlier distress forgotten as absolutely as if it had never existed.

"Look at that," Anong said. "Complete despair to complete contentment in thirty seconds. If only we could do that."

"We can't because we have memory," Somchai said. "The gift and curse of being adult is that we remember our suffering. We can't just let it go the way children do."

"Is that really a curse?" Prasert asked. "Seems to me that remembering suffering is what keeps us from repeating mistakes."

"Yes. But it also keeps us from experiencing joy fully. We're always waiting for the suffering to come back. Even when things are good, part of us is braced for them to become bad."

"That's just being realistic."

"Is it realistic? Or is it a kind of preemptive suffering? Suffering in advance for things that might never happen?"

---

Somchai leaned back in his chair and looked out the window at the familiar street below. It was a beautiful day—clear sky, warm sun, the kind of weather that made even Bangkok seem gentle.

"I want to tell you something," he said. "Something I've never told anyone."

The other two waited. Somchai's rare personal revelations were always worth the wait.

"When I was fifty-three," he continued, "I went through a period of—I don't know what to call it. Depression seems too clinical. Despair, perhaps. A deep, lasting despair that colored everything gray."

"I didn't know that," Anong said.

"No one did. I hid it well. I still taught my classes, still saw my friends, still went through all the motions of living. But inside, there was nothing. Just emptiness. The kind that makes you wonder why you bother getting out of bed."

"What caused it?" Prasert asked.

"I don't know. There was no specific event. One day I woke up and the meaning had drained out of everything. Food tasted like cardboard. Music was just noise. Even the beauty of the world—" Somchai gestured at the window, the sky, the light. "Even that seemed like a cruel joke. Here are all these beautiful things, and you can't feel any of them."

"How long did it last?"

"Nearly two years."

"Two years," Anong breathed. "How did you survive?"

"By waiting. That's all. I knew—intellectually, not emotionally—that feelings change. That the despair wouldn't last forever, even if it felt like it would. So I kept going through the motions, trusting that eventually the motions would become real again."

"And they did?"

Somchai nodded slowly. "One morning I woke up and the gray was a little lighter. A few days later, lighter still. It took months to fully lift, and even now, decades later, I can feel it sometimes—just a shadow at the edge of things. But it taught me something I've never forgotten."

"What?"

"That joy and suffering are both temporary. Neither lasts. The suffering feels eternal when you're in it, and so does the joy. But both are passing states, like weather. The skill isn't in trying to hold onto joy or escape suffering. It's in learning to let both of them move through you without resistance."

---

Prasert was frowning, processing this information in his engineer's way—trying to fit it into a framework that made sense.

"So you're saying happiness is pointless?" he said finally. "Because it's going to end anyway?"

"I'm saying the opposite. Happiness is precious precisely because it ends. If joy were permanent, it would become meaningless. It's the impermanence that gives it value."

"That's very philosophical."

"That's very true. Look at this moment, right now. We're here, together, alive, relatively comfortable. How many more moments like this will we have? A hundred? A thousand? It seems like a lot until you realize how quickly they pass."

Anong was looking at his friends with an expression that was hard to read—something between gratitude and grief.

"I almost didn't come today," he admitted. "I woke up feeling... heavy. Missing Noi. Thinking about how much longer I can keep doing this. Getting out of bed. Coming to this tea house. Pretending everything is fine."

"You don't have to pretend," Somchai said gently. "Not with us."

"I know. But sometimes the not-pretending feels worse. Like if I admit how hard it is, I'll fall apart completely."

Prasert reached across the table and put his hand on Anong's arm—awkwardly, because physical affection did not come naturally to him, but deliberately, because this was his friend and his friend was hurting.

"We're here," Prasert said. "For whatever that's worth."

"It's worth everything," Anong said. "That's what I was trying to explain. This morning was suffering. Real suffering, the kind that makes you want to stay in bed forever. But this—" he looked at their hands, the table, the tea, the window with its familiar light. "This is joy. And they're happening right next to each other. Not separately. Together."

---

The toddler at the nearby table had started whining again—a cyclical misery that seemed to have no beginning or end. His mother looked exhausted, that particular exhaustion of young parents who can't remember what sleep or silence feel like.

"Should we help?" Anong asked.

"With what?" Prasert said. "We're three old men. What do we know about children?"

"We've all raised children."

"And we all did it wrong. Different wrong, but still wrong."

Despite himself, Somchai smiled. "He's not entirely incorrect."

"I'm never entirely incorrect. I'm occasionally partially correct, which is the best any of us can hope for."

The mother caught Anong's eye and gave a rueful shrug—the universal gesture of parental resignation. Anong smiled back, wanting to tell her that it got easier, that the children grew up and the exhaustion faded and eventually you'd miss even this.

But he didn't. Because it wasn't entirely true. The children grew up and became other kinds of difficult. The exhaustion changed forms but never really disappeared. And you didn't miss this—you missed the version of this that existed only in memory, smoothed over and made golden by time.

"Every stage of life has its suffering," Somchai said, as if reading his thoughts. "And every stage has its joy. The mistake is thinking that some other stage would be better. That if we could only get to the next phase, or go back to the previous one, happiness would finally be available."

"The grass is always greener," Prasert said.

"The grass is always grass. We're the ones who keep painting it different colors."

---

The afternoon was deepening, and the tea house had filled with its usual mix of characters—the retired businessmen comparing portfolios, the young couples ignoring each other for their phones, the elderly women gossiping over small cups of expensive tea.

"I had a dream last night," Anong said. "About Noi. She was standing in our kitchen, making breakfast, and I was sitting at the table watching her. And I knew—in the dream, I knew—that she was dead. That this was just a memory or a vision or whatever dreams are. But I also felt completely happy. More happy than I've felt since she died."

"Because she was there," Somchai said.

"Because she was there, and I was there, and for one moment—one precious, impossible moment—we were together again." Anong's voice caught. "I woke up crying. But the strange thing is, I didn't feel sad. I felt... grateful. Like the universe had given me a gift I didn't deserve."

"Why wouldn't you deserve it?" Prasert asked.

"Because I had forty-seven years with her. Forty-seven years of waking up next to her, eating breakfast with her, arguing with her about stupid things and making up afterward. So many years. And other people—people who love just as much as I did—they get a few months. A few years if they're lucky. Why should I get even one more moment when I've already had more than my share?"

"It doesn't work that way," Somchai said. "Love isn't a limited resource that runs out if you use too much. The universe doesn't keep track of who deserves what."

"Then how does it work?"

"I don't know. I don't think anyone knows. But I know that the joy of that dream was real. The grief of waking up was real. And both of them are part of loving her."

---

Prasert had been quiet, listening with the intensity he usually reserved for structural problems.

"I don't dream about Mei," he said finally. "My wife. I haven't dreamed about her once since she died. Fifteen years, and not one dream."

"That doesn't mean anything," Anong said.

"Doesn't it? Fifteen years, and my subconscious has completely forgotten her? What does that say about our marriage?"

"It says your subconscious is a terrible measure of anything," Somchai said. "I dream about people I met once, thirty years ago, whose names I can't remember. I've never once dreamed about my mother. That doesn't mean I loved her less."

"Maybe not. But sometimes I wonder—" Prasert stopped, started again. "Sometimes I wonder if I ever really loved her at all. Or if I just performed love well enough that neither of us noticed the difference."

"There is no difference," Anong said firmly. "Love performed is still love. The intention doesn't matter as much as the action."

"That's very pragmatic of you."

"I'm a pragmatist. Always have been. I don't care why people are kind to me—I just care that they're kind."

"But don't you want to be loved for who you actually are?" Prasert asked. "Not for who you're pretending to be?"

"There's no one I actually am," Anong said. "Not really. I'm different with different people. The me who talked to Noi isn't the same me who talks to you. The me who ran a business isn't the same me who plays with grandchildren. We're all made up of masks. The trick is finding people who love the masks."

"That's depressing."

"That's liberating. It means you don't have to find your 'true self' to be worthy of love. You just have to show up as whoever you are in that moment, and hope someone shows up back."

---

The sun was beginning its descent, painting the tea house in shades of amber that made everything look soft and old and precious.

"I think we're too hard on ourselves," Somchai said. "About happiness. About love. About all of it. We hold ourselves to standards that no one could meet—perfect joy, perfect love, perfect understanding—and then we feel like failures when we fall short."

"Aren't we failures?" Prasert asked, without self-pity—just genuine curiosity.

"At what? At being perfect? Certainly. At being human? No. We've done exactly what humans do. We've loved imperfectly. Suffered imperfectly. Found joy in unexpected places and lost it in expected ones. That's not failure. That's life."

"It feels like failure sometimes."

"Feelings aren't facts. You taught me that, Prasert. Years ago, when I was going through my despair. You said: 'Your feelings are lying to you. The math doesn't support them.'"

Prasert smiled slightly. "That sounds like something I'd say."

"It helped. More than you know. Feelings lie to us all the time—especially feelings about our own worth. The skill is learning to question them without dismissing them entirely."

"A fine line."

"Most wisdom is."

---

As they prepared to leave—the slow gathering of belongings, the careful rising from chairs, the negotiation of who would pay this week—Anong turned to his friends with a look of unusual seriousness.

"Can I ask you both something?"

"Of course."

"Are you happy? Right now, today, in this moment—are you happy?"

The question hung in the air, heavier than it should have been.

Prasert spoke first. "I don't know. I'm not unhappy. Is that the same thing?"

"Maybe. Somchai?"

Somchai considered the question with his usual care. "In this moment, yes. I'm with friends. The tea is good. The light is beautiful. I'm aware that I'm alive, which is more than I can say for most moments."

"And beyond this moment?"

"Beyond this moment is beyond my concern. The future will contain whatever it contains. I'm trying to learn to meet it without anticipation."

"That sounds enlightened."

"It sounds like something I'm working on. The truth is, I'm terrified of what's coming. The body failing. The mind fading. The long slide toward an end I can't predict or control. But I'm trying not to let that fear steal the joy that's available now."

Anong nodded slowly. "That's what I'm trying to do too. And mostly failing. But trying."

"The trying is the practice," Somchai said. "The failing is the learning. There is no final achievement of happiness. There's only the daily attempt to be present for whatever shows up."

---

They walked out into the evening together, three old men who had argued about happiness without reaching any conclusions—which was, perhaps, the only honest way to discuss the subject.

The city hummed around them, indifferent and beautiful and continuing on as it always had, as it always would, long after they were gone.

"Same time next week?" Anong asked.

"If we're still here," Prasert said.

"We'll be here," Somchai said. "One way or another."

They parted at the corner, each carrying their own particular mixture of joy and suffering, their own private attempts to make sense of a world that refused to be sensible.

And somewhere in the city, a little girl was still trying to walk her cat on a leash.

And the cat was still winning.

And life, despite everything, continued.

---

*End of Chapter Four*
