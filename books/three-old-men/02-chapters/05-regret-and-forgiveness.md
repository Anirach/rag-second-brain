# Chapter Five
## Regret and Forgiveness

*In which old wounds are examined and some are finally allowed to heal*

---

There was a new crack in the ceiling of the tea house—or perhaps it had always been there, and Somchai was only now noticing it. The line ran from the corner near the window to somewhere above the counter, a thin dark thread in the old plaster that spoke of settling, of age, of the slow inevitable process by which all things return to the earth.

"You're staring at the ceiling," Prasert observed.

"I'm watching it change," Somchai replied. "Though the change is too slow to actually see."

"That's not watching. That's imagining."

"Is there a difference?"

Prasert opened his mouth to argue, then closed it. After thirty years of friendship, he recognized the conversations that led nowhere productive.

Anong arrived late, slightly breathless, carrying a cardboard box that seemed to contain something precious—or at least something he was treating as precious, holding it carefully in both hands as he navigated between the tables.

"What's that?" Prasert asked, suspicious as always of unannounced objects.

"My past," Anong said, setting the box on the table between them. "Or at least some of it. I was cleaning out the storage room, and I found this."

He opened the box to reveal a jumble of photographs—old ones, the kind that were printed on thick paper and had wavy edges and colors that had faded to shades of orange and brown.

"Family photos?" Somchai asked.

"Everything photos. My whole life, apparently, thrown into a box and forgotten for decades." Anong began spreading the photographs on the table, arranging them with the absent-minded care of a man reacquainting himself with ghosts.

"Here's Noi and me at our wedding—look how young we were. And this is my father, standing in front of the original fishing boat. And this—" he paused, picking up a photograph with a complicated expression. "This is my brother."

The other two waited. Anong rarely mentioned his brother.

"Sakorn. Three years older than me. We haven't spoken in thirty-seven years."

"What happened?" Somchai asked, though he had heard pieces of this story before, scattered across decades of friendship like shards of a broken vase.

Anong held the photograph closer to his face, as if trying to read something in his brother's young features. "The business happened. Our father died, left the fishing company to both of us equally. Sakorn wanted to modernize—new boats, new equipment, loans that made me nervous. I wanted to stay small, stay careful, stay safe."

"And?"

"And we fought. For two years we fought about everything—money, strategy, each other's character. It got ugly. Things were said that couldn't be unsaid. Eventually, he took his half and started his own company, and we never spoke again."

"Does he still live in Bangkok?" Prasert asked.

"Chiang Mai. Or at least, that's where he was the last I heard. He could be dead for all I know. I could be mourning a man who's already been mourned."

The photograph sat on the table between them—two young men, arms around each other's shoulders, grinning at the camera with the easy confidence of people who couldn't imagine ever being enemies.

---

"Why did you bring this?" Somchai asked. "The box, the photos. Why today?"

Anong was quiet for a long moment, arranging and rearranging the photographs in patterns that seemed to have meaning only to him.

"Because I'm old," he said finally. "Because I might not have many years left to decide what to do about this. Because I woke up this morning thinking about regrets, and Sakorn is the biggest regret of my life."

"Bigger than not being there when your father died?"

"That wasn't my fault—I was on a boat, three days from shore. But Sakorn—" Anong shook his head. "Sakorn I did myself. I chose anger over love. I chose being right over being brothers. And I've had thirty-seven years to regret it."

"Have you thought about reaching out?" Somchai asked gently. "Making contact?"

"Every day. Every single day, I think about picking up the phone. And every day, I don't."

"Why not?"

"Because I'm afraid." Anong's voice was barely above a whisper. "I'm afraid he'll hang up. I'm afraid he'll tell me it's too late. I'm afraid he'll say the things I've been saying to myself all these years—that I'm weak, that I'm a coward, that I destroyed our family for nothing."

"And if he doesn't say those things?"

"Then I've wasted thirty-seven years being afraid for nothing. Which might be worse."

---

Prasert had been listening with the uncomfortable expression of a man confronted with emotions he didn't know how to process. But something in Anong's confession had loosened something in him.

"My father never forgave me," he said suddenly.

The other two looked at him.

"For what?" Anong asked.

"For becoming an engineer instead of taking over his hardware store. For moving to Bangkok instead of staying in the village. For being—" Prasert made a gesture that encompassed himself, his life, all his choices. "For being who I am instead of who he wanted me to be."

"He must have been proud eventually," Somchai said. "When you built the bridges. The buildings."

"He never said so. He died when I was forty-two, and the last time we spoke, he called me an ungrateful son who had abandoned his family for foreign ideas." Prasert's voice was flat, the way it got when he was working hard to control it. "I didn't go to his funeral. I was working on a project in Cambodia, and I told myself I couldn't leave. But the truth is, I didn't want to face the village. Didn't want to see the accusation in everyone's eyes."

"That's a heavy thing to carry," Somchai said.

"Thirty-six years. Slightly shorter than Anong's record." Prasert smiled, but there was no humor in it. "I tell myself it doesn't matter anymore. He's dead. His opinions are dust. But sometimes I wake up in the middle of the night, and I can hear him saying: 'This is what you left me for? This is what was so important?'"

"What would you say to him?" Anong asked. "If you could speak to him now?"

Prasert was quiet for a long time. The tea house noise swirled around them—other conversations, the clink of cups, the distant sound of traffic—but their table had become an island of stillness.

"I would say I'm sorry," Prasert said finally. "Not for my choices. I still think I was right to leave. But for the way I left. For the years of silence. For not being there when he was sick, when he was dying, when he needed his son and his son was too proud to come."

"You were afraid too," Somchai observed. "Of confrontation. Of judgment. Of finding out that his love was conditional."

"His love was conditional. That's not a fear—that's a fact."

"Is it? Or is that what you told yourself to justify the distance?"

Prasert didn't answer.

---

The afternoon light was shifting, and Somchai noticed that the crack in the ceiling caught the sun in a way that made it almost beautiful—a thread of gold in the worn plaster.

"I have regrets too," he said, surprising himself. He hadn't planned to share this.

"You?" Prasert seemed genuinely startled. "You're the most accepting person I know. You practically invented letting go."

"I practice letting go because I know how tight my grip used to be." Somchai picked up his tea cup, set it down again. "There was a student I failed. Years ago, when I was still teaching. A young man with problems I didn't understand—we didn't have words for it then. Depression, perhaps. Anxiety. Something that made him fragile in ways his classmates weren't."

"What happened?"

"He struggled. Failed exams. Stopped coming to class. I tried to reach him—or I told myself I tried. Looking back, I think I was going through the motions. I was tired. I had thirty other students who also needed help. I couldn't save everyone."

"That's reasonable," Anong said.

"Reasonable, yes. Right, perhaps not." Somchai's voice was soft. "He took his own life at the end of the semester. Jumped from a building on campus. He was twenty years old."

The silence that followed was heavy with things that couldn't be said.

"That wasn't your fault," Prasert said finally.

"I know that. Intellectually. But regret isn't about logic. I keep thinking: what if I had pushed harder? What if I had noticed the warning signs? What if I had told him that his struggles didn't define him, that there was help available, that life was worth continuing?"

"You couldn't have known," Anong said.

"Maybe not. But I should have tried harder to know. That's my regret. Not that I failed to save him—I probably couldn't have. But that I didn't try hard enough to see him."

---

The three old men sat with their regrets spread between them like the photographs on the table—images of past failures, frozen in time, impossible to change but impossible to forget.

"Is forgiveness possible?" Anong asked. "Real forgiveness, I mean. Not just saying the words."

"What do you think forgiveness is?" Somchai asked.

"I don't know. That's why I'm asking."

Somchai considered this. "I think forgiveness is letting go of the demand that the past be different. It's accepting what happened without continuing to punish yourself or others for it."

"That sounds like giving up."

"It sounds like freedom. The past is finished. It can't be changed. Every moment we spend relitigating it is a moment we're not present for the life we're actually living."

"But how do you do it?" Prasert demanded. "How do you just... let go of something that still hurts?"

"You don't do it just once. You do it over and over. Every time the memory surfaces, every time the old anger or grief rises up, you practice letting go again. It's not an achievement—it's a practice."

"That sounds exhausting."

"It is. But not as exhausting as holding on forever."

---

Anong had picked up the photograph of his brother again. He was looking at it with an expression that seemed to combine love and loss in equal measure.

"I'm going to call him," he said suddenly. "Sakorn. I'm going to find his number and call him."

"Now?" Prasert asked.

"Not this exact moment. But soon. Before I lose my nerve. Before I convince myself that the silence is safer than the conversation."

"What will you say?"

"I don't know. I'll start with 'I'm sorry' and see where it goes." Anong looked up at his friends. "I've been waiting for him to make the first move. For thirty-seven years, I've been waiting for him to apologize, to reach out, to admit he was wrong. And today I realized: I might die waiting. We both might die waiting. And then what was the point of any of it?"

"He might not forgive you," Somchai said gently. "He might hang up. He might say terrible things."

"I know. But at least I'll have tried. At least I'll know I didn't let pride keep me from my brother for my entire life."

"That's brave," Prasert said, and he sounded like he meant it.

"It's terrifying. But I'm old enough now to know that courage and fear aren't opposites. The bravest things I've ever done were the things that scared me most."

---

The conversation drifted after that, as conversations do—to lighter topics, to complaints about weather and politics, to the comfortable rhythms of old friendship. But something had shifted. The confessions they had shared had created a new intimacy, a new vulnerability that none of them quite knew how to hold.

As the afternoon wore on, Prasert made an announcement of his own.

"I'm going to write to my son," he said. "A real letter. Not a voicemail he might delete, but a letter he has to hold in his hands."

"What will you write?"

"Everything I should have said decades ago. An apology for pushing him away. An acknowledgment that he was right to follow his own path. A request—not a demand—for a chance to know my grandchildren before I die."

"You think he'll respond?"

"I don't know. But I'm done letting uncertainty be an excuse for inaction." Prasert looked down at his hands, the hands that had built bridges across impossible rivers. "I've spent my whole life building things that would outlast me. But the only things that really last are the connections between people. Everything else is just... architecture."

"Very philosophical for an engineer," Somchai said.

"I have philosophical friends. Some of it must have rubbed off."

---

Somchai had been quiet, listening to his friends make their resolutions. Now he spoke, almost to himself.

"I'm going to visit the campus where my student died. I've avoided it for twenty years. I've told myself it was out of the way, or that I was too busy, or that nothing good could come from revisiting old pain. But I think—" he paused, finding the words. "I think I need to say goodbye. To stand in the place where he ended and tell him I'm sorry I couldn't do more. Even if he can't hear me. Even if it changes nothing."

"Why now?" Anong asked.

"Because we're running out of time to do the things we've been postponing. And some things shouldn't be postponed." Somchai looked at the photograph of Anong's brother, still lying on the table. "We carry our regrets around like stones in our pockets. They weigh us down, slow us down, keep us anchored to a past that no longer exists. Maybe it's time to set them down."

"And if they've become part of us?" Prasert asked. "If we've carried them so long we don't know who we are without them?"

"Then we find out. We become whoever we become without the weight. Maybe that person will be lighter. Maybe they'll be lost. But at least they'll be honest."

---

The sun was setting now, filling the tea house with that golden light that made everything look softer, more forgivable. Anong gathered up his photographs, putting them back in the box with careful reverence.

"Thank you," he said. "For listening. For sharing. For being the kind of friends who don't judge."

"We judge constantly," Prasert said. "We're just polite enough not to say it out loud."

"That's all I ask."

They rose to leave—the slow, careful rising that had become their custom—and made their way toward the door. Outside, the city was coming alive with the energy of evening, that particular Bangkok magic of light and sound and smell that never got old no matter how many decades you'd experienced it.

"Same time next week?" Anong asked.

"Assuming we're still speaking to each other," Prasert said. "After all this emotional honesty, I might need a break."

"You love the emotional honesty."

"I tolerate the emotional honesty. There's a difference."

But he was smiling, and they all knew that tolerance was just his word for love.

They parted at the corner, each carrying their regrets a little lighter, each walking toward conversations they had postponed too long.

And somewhere in Chiang Mai, a phone that had been silent for thirty-seven years might soon ring.

And somewhere in California, a letter might soon arrive.

And somewhere in the past, a young man who had felt unseen might finally be acknowledged.

Forgiveness, it turns out, isn't about the other person at all.

It's about finally deciding to put down the weight.

---

*End of Chapter Five*
