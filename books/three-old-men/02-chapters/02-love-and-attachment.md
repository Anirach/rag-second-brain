# Chapter Two
## Love and Attachment

*In which they remember the women who shaped them and the children who surprised them*

---

The rain came without warning, as rain does in Bangkok—one moment the sky was merely gray, the next it was falling in sheets that turned the streets into rivers and sent pedestrians scrambling for cover under awnings and into doorways.

Inside the tea house, the three old men watched the deluge through the window with the calm detachment of people who had nowhere they needed to be.

"Good day for it," Anong said, meaning the tea, meaning the company, meaning this small moment of shelter while the world outside tried to drown itself.

"Good day for staying inside," Prasert agreed, which was as close as he came to poetry.

Somchai simply smiled and poured himself more jasmine tea.

The rain drummed against the old roof, finding leaks that had been there for decades and would likely be there for decades more. A slow drip had started in the corner, falling into a bucket that the waiter had placed there with the weary expertise of long practice.

"It rained like this the day I met Noi," Anong said, as if the memory had surfaced unbidden—which it had, as memories do, triggered by weather and light and the particular quality of this moment.

The other two waited. They had heard this story before, but stories improve with repetition, and besides, what else was there to do while the world drowned outside?

"I was twenty-three," Anong continued. "Young and stupid and convinced I was neither. I had just taken over my father's fishing business—one boat, held together with rope and prayers—and I thought I was the most important man in the province."

"You haven't changed much," Prasert observed.

"I've gotten humbler. Slightly." Anong smiled at the memory. "Anyway, it was raining like this, and I was running through the market trying to find shelter, and I crashed right into her. Literally crashed. Sent her vegetables everywhere. Cabbages rolling down the street like little green heads."

"Romantic," Prasert said.

"It was terrible. She was furious. She called me a water buffalo with feet for brains—which, looking back, was fair. And I just stood there, soaking wet, watching her pick up her cabbages, and I thought: I'm going to marry that woman."

"Love at first insult," Somchai said.

"The best kind. Anyone can fall in love with someone who's nice to you. But when someone calls you a water buffalo and you still want to spend your life with them—that's when you know it's real."

"Did you help her pick up the cabbages, at least?"

"Eventually. After I finished staring like an idiot." Anong's eyes were distant now, looking at something the others couldn't see. "She had this way of looking at you—like she could see everything you were trying to hide. Forty-seven years of marriage, and she never lost that look. Right up until the end, she could still see through me completely."

The rain continued its assault on the roof. In the street below, a lone motorcyclist was attempting to navigate the flooded road, his engine coughing in protest.

"I didn't marry for love," Prasert said suddenly.

The other two looked at him with surprise. Prasert rarely talked about his wife, who had died fifteen years ago after a long illness that neither of them discussed.

"My parents arranged it," he continued, his voice carefully neutral. "Mei's family had land, my family had debts, and that was that. I met her twice before the wedding. We barely spoke."

"But you learned to love her," Anong said. It wasn't a question.

Prasert was quiet for a long moment. "I learned to... appreciate her. To respect her. To rely on her. Is that love? I don't know. We were together for forty-two years, and I still don't know."

"It sounds like love to me," Somchai said gently.

"That's because you romanticize everything." But there was no bite in Prasert's words. "Mei was practical. Sensible. She never expected me to be something I wasn't. She never complained when I worked late or forgot anniversaries or failed to say the things a husband is supposed to say."

"What things?"

Prasert shrugged uncomfortably. "You know. The things in movies. 'I love you.' 'You're beautiful.' 'I don't know what I'd do without you.'"

"Did you never say those things?"

"Once. At the end. When it didn't matter anymore." Prasert picked up his cup of hot water and stared into it. "She was in the hospital, and the doctors had told me she probably wouldn't make it through the night. And I sat there holding her hand, and I said all the things I should have been saying for forty-two years."

The silence that followed was heavy with things unspoken.

"What did she say?" Anong asked quietly.

"She said: 'I know. I always knew.'" Prasert's voice cracked slightly, then steadied. "And then she died. Just like that. Between one breath and the next."

"That's a good way to go," Somchai said. "Knowing you were loved. Knowing you had loved."

"Is it? Sometimes I think I should have said those things earlier. Given her years of hearing them instead of one night."

"The saying isn't the important part," Somchai said. "The living is. You showed her love for forty-two years, even if you didn't say it. Every time you came home instead of staying at work. Every time you sat with her when she was sick. Every time you let her win an argument because her happiness mattered more than being right."

Prasert looked up sharply. "How do you know about that?"

"Because that's what love looks like. Not grand gestures and poetry. Just showing up, day after day, choosing someone over and over again."

---

The rain was beginning to ease, though the streets would stay flooded for hours yet. The tea house had filled up with refugees from the storm—a young couple sharing an umbrella, a businessman in a wet suit, an old woman with a small dog tucked inside her jacket.

"And you, Somchai?" Anong asked. "You never married. But surely there was someone?"

Somchai was quiet for so long that Anong began to regret asking. But finally, the old teacher spoke.

"Her name was Lalita," he said. "We were together for three years, when I was teaching in Chiang Mai. She was a nurse at the hospital there—kind, gentle, with a laugh that could light up a room."

"What happened?"

"Life happened." Somchai's voice was soft, without bitterness. "She wanted children. A home. The ordinary things that ordinary people want. And I—I wanted something I couldn't name. Something that made me restless, that sent me wandering from city to city, from job to job, always searching for something I couldn't find."

"Did you find it?" Prasert asked.

"Eventually. But by then, she had married someone else. Had children. Built the life she wanted with a man who was willing to stay."

"Do you regret it?"

Somchai considered the question with the seriousness he gave to all questions. "I regret the pain I caused her. The confusion. The years she spent waiting for me to become something I wasn't. But the choice itself—" he shook his head slowly. "No. I don't regret that. We are what we are. Pretending otherwise only causes more suffering."

"That sounds very philosophical," Anong said. "Also very lonely."

"It was lonely. For a long time. But loneliness isn't the worst thing." Somchai smiled slightly. "The worst thing is being with someone and feeling lonely. At least when you're alone, you know where you stand."

"That's surprisingly cynical for you," Prasert observed.

"It's not cynical—it's honest. I've seen too many marriages where both people are miserable, staying together out of duty or fear or just habit. That's lonelier than any empty room."

"So you chose solitude over compromise."

"I chose truth over comfort. Which doesn't make me better than either of you. Just different."

---

Anong was watching a young couple at a nearby table. They were in their twenties, probably, leaning toward each other, speaking in low voices, their hands intertwined on the table between them. They had that look—the look of people who believed their love was unique, unprecedented, a miracle the world had never seen before.

"Look at them," Anong said. "Remember when we were like that?"

"I was never like that," Prasert said. "Mei and I didn't hold hands in public. It wasn't done."

"But you felt it. The excitement. The certainty. The feeling that nothing could possibly go wrong because love would protect you."

"That's naivete, not love."

"Is there a difference? When you're young, I mean?"

Somchai watched the young couple with an expression of gentle melancholy. "They don't know what's coming," he said. "They think love is a destination. Somewhere you arrive and stay forever."

"And it's not?"

"Love is a verb. Something you do every day. Something you have to choose again and again, even when it's difficult. Especially when it's difficult." He took a sip of his tea. "That couple will face things they can't imagine right now. Illness. Financial troubles. The slow grinding wear of two different people trying to share one life. And some of them—the lucky ones—will discover that love isn't what they thought it was. It's not a feeling. It's a practice."

"You should write fortune cookies," Prasert said.

"I considered it. The pay is terrible."

The young couple laughed at something, their joy filling the tea house like sudden sunlight. Anong smiled at the sound.

"I miss that," he said. "The laughing. Noi and I used to laugh all the time. Stupid things, private jokes that no one else would understand. I'd forgotten that, somehow. How much we laughed."

"Laughter is the first thing to go," Somchai said. "When a relationship is struggling, the laughter disappears before anything else."

"And when it comes back?" Anong asked.

"Then you know you've survived something together."

---

The conversation drifted, as conversations do, from love to children, from children to regrets, from regrets back to love again. The rain had stopped entirely now, and steam was rising from the wet streets, giving the city a ghostly, ethereal quality.

"My son doesn't speak to me," Prasert said abruptly.

The other two waited. This, too, they had heard before, but the wound was still fresh even after all these years, and fresh wounds need air.

"Five years now. He lives in America—California—with his wife and my grandchildren, whom I've never met. He sends a card at New Year. Sometimes a photo. But we don't talk."

"What happened?" Anong asked, though he knew the answer.

"I happened. I pushed him too hard. Wanted him to be an engineer, like me. Wanted him to take over my projects, continue my work. And he—" Prasert's voice tightened. "He wanted to paint. To make art. Things I didn't understand and didn't try to understand."

"And now?"

"Now he's a successful artist in Los Angeles, and I'm an old man who will probably die without ever meeting his grandchildren." Prasert's hands were tight around his cup. "The worst part is, I know I was wrong. I've known for years. But I don't know how to say it. I don't know how to reach across that distance and tell him—"

"Tell him what?" Somchai asked gently.

"That I'm proud of him. That I was always proud of him. That I just didn't know how to show it."

"Write him a letter," Anong suggested. "Say exactly that."

"He wouldn't read it."

"You don't know that."

"I know my son." Prasert's voice was bitter. "He got his stubbornness from me. He'll hold onto that anger until one of us is dead. Probably until both of us are dead."

"Pride is expensive," Somchai observed. "The most expensive thing there is. We pay for it with our relationships, our happiness, our peace of mind. And in the end, what does it buy us? An empty room and a clear conscience that keeps us awake at night."

"I'm not sure my conscience is clear," Prasert admitted.

"That's actually a good sign. If your conscience were clear, you wouldn't have learned anything."

---

Anong had been thinking about his own children—four of them, scattered across the country, calling on holidays, visiting when they could, slowly building their own lives in which he played an increasingly minor role.

"My daughter keeps trying to get me to move in with her," he said. "She has a big house in Chiang Mai. A garden. A room ready just for me, she says. Good air, clean water, no traffic."

"Why don't you go?" Prasert asked.

"Because I'm not ready to become someone's burden." Anong's voice was firm despite his age. "I've spent my whole life being useful. Building things. Providing for people. I'm not ready to become the person who needs to be provided for."

"That's pride too," Somchai pointed out.

"Maybe. But it's my pride, and I'm keeping it until I can't anymore."

"When will that be?"

Anong looked at his hands—hands that had hauled nets and counted money and held his wife as she took her last breath. "I don't know. When I can't get to this tea house on my own anymore, maybe. When I forget your names. When the burden of caring for me outweighs the gift of my company." He smiled sadly. "I hope I'll know when that moment comes. I hope I'll have the courage to stop fighting."

"My mother didn't," Somchai said quietly. "She wanted to die at home, in her own bed, with her things around her. But the cancer was too aggressive. They took her to the hospital for pain management, and she never came back."

"Did she know?"

"She knew. The last thing she said to me was: 'Don't let them keep you in a place like this.' And then—" Somchai's voice caught briefly. "Then she was gone."

The three men sat in silence, each lost in their own memories of lost mothers, lost wives, lost pieces of themselves that would never come back.

"We're a cheerful bunch," Prasert said finally.

"We're an honest bunch," Anong corrected. "Which is worth more, at our age."

---

The young couple at the nearby table was getting up to leave, still holding hands, still glowing with that particular light of new love. As they passed, the young woman smiled at Anong—a warm, genuine smile from a stranger.

"Thank you," she said, surprising everyone.

"For what?" Anong asked.

"For giving us hope. It's nice to see people who've been friends for so long. It makes us think maybe we'll make it too."

Anong didn't know what to say. He watched them leave, hands still intertwined, stepping out into the wet street with the carelessness of youth and health.

"We gave them hope," he said wonderingly. "Three old men complaining about their lives gave someone hope."

"We're not complaining," Somchai said. "We're processing. There's a difference."

"Is there?"

"Complaining is rejecting what is. Processing is accepting what is while learning from it."

"I thought we were just drinking tea," Prasert muttered.

"Tea is philosophy. Philosophy is tea. It's all the same river."

"Your metaphors are getting worse with age."

"Everything gets worse with age. That's the point."

---

As the afternoon wore on, the conversation turned to lighter things—gossip about neighbors, complaints about politics, debates about which tea house had the best dim sum (a question that could never be satisfactorily resolved because each man had his own irreplaceable memories tied to specific places).

But underneath it all, the earlier talk of love still lingered, like the smell of rain on hot pavement.

"Do you think it's possible to love someone completely?" Anong asked during a lull. "Without reservation? Without holding anything back?"

Prasert considered this. "That sounds dangerous."

"That sounds necessary," Somchai countered. "Half-hearted love is worse than no love at all. At least with no love, you know where you stand."

"So you've said."

"So I keep learning. The lesson doesn't stick."

Anong was nodding slowly. "I think I loved Noi completely. Or at least, as completely as I was capable of. There was nothing I wouldn't have done for her. Nothing I wouldn't have given up."

"Would you have given up your pride?" Prasert asked, and his voice was carefully neutral. "Your sense of self? Your independence?"

"I did. Gladly. That's what love asks of you. To let go of the things that keep you separate. To become part of something larger than yourself."

"That sounds like drowning."

"It felt like freedom."

Somchai was quiet, listening to his friends with an expression that was hard to read.

"Both things can be true," he said finally. "Love can be freedom and drowning. Salvation and destruction. The same fire that warms you can burn your house down."

"So what's the answer?" Prasert demanded. "Love completely and risk destruction? Or hold back and risk nothing meaningful?"

"The answer is different for everyone. For some people, the risk is worth it. For others, the preservation of self matters more. Neither is wrong."

"That's a non-answer."

"Most honest answers are."

---

The sun was beginning to set, painting the rain-washed city in shades of gold and pink. The streets had mostly dried, leaving only puddles that reflected the sky like scattered pieces of mirror.

"Same time next week?" Anong asked as they began their slow ritual of departure.

"Same time," Somchai confirmed.

"Assuming the world doesn't end before then," Prasert added, because pessimism was his love language.

"The world has been ending since we were born," Anong said. "And yet here we are."

"Here we are," Somchai agreed. "Still talking about love. Still trying to understand something that probably can't be understood."

"Speak for yourself. I understand love perfectly."

"Really, Prasert? Enlighten us."

Prasert stood, steadying himself on his cane—a concession to age that he still resented even after ten years of using it.

"Love is showing up," he said simply. "That's all. You show up when you don't want to. You show up when it's hard. You show up when you're angry, or tired, or convinced the other person is wrong about everything. And you keep showing up, day after day, until one of you can't anymore."

He looked at his friends—these two men he had known for thirty years, with whom he had shared more of himself than he had shared with anyone, including his wife.

"That's what we do," he said. "Every week. We show up."

"That's the most romantic thing you've ever said," Anong said.

"It wasn't meant to be romantic."

"I know. That's what makes it romantic."

They walked out together into the evening air, three old men who had loved and lost and somehow kept going. The city hummed around them, indifferent to their small dramas, their decades of history, their complicated hearts.

But they didn't need the city to care.

They had each other.

And for now, that was enough.

---

*End of Chapter Two*
