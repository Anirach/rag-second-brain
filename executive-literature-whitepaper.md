# Should Executives Read Literary Fiction?
## A White Paper on Strategic Thinking Under Uncertainty

Prepared by Arthur for Anirach

January 2026

---

## Executive Summary

This white paper examines the compelling case for why executives and strategic leaders should incorporate classic literary fiction into their professional development. While business schools emphasize case studies with known outcomes, literary fiction offers something fundamentally different—and arguably more valuable—for navigating real-world strategic uncertainty.

Classic literature does not offer solutions. It forces readers to tolerate ambiguity. It trains long-horizon thinking and second-order consequences. This mirrors real executive decision-making far more accurately than case studies with predetermined answers.

---

## 🔬 The Research Foundation

### Reading Fiction Improves Theory of Mind

A landmark study published in Science by researchers at The New School (Castano & Kidd, 2013) found that reading literary fiction significantly improves a reader's capacity to understand what others are thinking and feeling—a cognitive skill known as "Theory of Mind" (ToM).

Theory of Mind is the ability to attribute mental states—beliefs, intentions, desires, emotions—to oneself and others, and to understand that others have beliefs and intentions different from one's own. This capacity is fundamental to executive leadership, negotiation, stakeholder management, and strategic communication.

**Key Finding:** Participants who read literary fiction showed marked improvement in Theory of Mind tests compared to those who read popular fiction, non-fiction, or nothing at all. [1]

### Literary Fiction vs. Genre Fiction: Why It Matters

Popular fiction tends to portray situations that follow a formula with internally consistent and predictable characters. Literary fiction, by contrast, focuses on the psychology of characters and their relationships, with minds depicted vaguely, forcing readers to fill in gaps to understand motivations and intentions. [1]

As researcher David Kidd explains: "Often those characters' minds are depicted vaguely, without many details, and we're forced to fill in the gaps to understand their intentions and motivations." [1]

### Neural Evidence: Reading Changes the Brain

Research from Emory University (Berns et al., 2013) using fMRI brain scans found that reading novels enhances connectivity in the brain, particularly in areas associated with:
- Language comprehension (left temporal cortex)
- Sensorimotor function (central sulcus)
- Embodied cognition—the ability to mentally simulate experiences [2]

"The neural changes that we found associated with physical sensation and movement systems suggest that reading a novel can transport you into the body of the protagonist," said neuroscientist Professor Gregory S. Berns. [2]

Critically, these neural changes persisted for days after reading, suggesting lasting cognitive benefits.

## 📊 Why Case Studies Fall Short

### The Problem with Known Outcomes

Business school case studies are valuable pedagogical tools, but they suffer from a fundamental limitation: the outcome is known. Students analyze decisions with hindsight, knowing whether the strategy succeeded or failed.

Real executive decision-making happens in the fog of uncertainty. When a CEO must decide whether to:
- Enter a new market
- Acquire a competitor
- Pivot business strategy
- Navigate a crisis

...there is no answer key. There is no professor to reveal the "correct" analysis.

### Literature Mirrors Reality

Classic literature offers what case studies cannot:

**1. No Solutions Provided**
Great novels do not tell you what to think. They present complex situations and leave interpretation to the reader. Was Hamlet right to delay? Was Anna Karenina's tragedy inevitable? These questions have no definitive answers—just as real strategic dilemmas resist simple solutions.

**2. Tolerance for Ambiguity**
Literary fiction forces readers to sit with uncertainty. Characters' motivations remain unclear. Plot threads resolve ambiguously. This cognitive workout builds the mental resilience executives need when facing incomplete information and uncertain outcomes.

**3. Long-Horizon Thinking**
Great novels unfold across generations, tracking how decisions ripple through time. One Hundred Years of Solitude spans seven generations. War and Peace covers decades. This trains the mind to think beyond quarterly results to second-order and third-order consequences.

**4. Second-Order Consequences**
In Dostoevsky's Crime and Punishment, a single decision—a murder committed for "rational" reasons—cascades into psychological, social, and spiritual consequences the protagonist never anticipated. Executives who read such works develop intuition for how actions propagate through complex systems.

## 🎯 Recommended Reading: Literature for Strategic Thinkers

### For Tolerating Ambiguity

**The Brothers Karamazov** by Fyodor Dostoevsky
A philosophical novel exploring faith, doubt, morality, and free will through three brothers with radically different worldviews. There are no heroes, no villains—only humans grappling with impossible questions. Executives learn that competing perspectives can all contain truth.

**Relevance:** Teaches that moral and strategic ambiguity is irreducible; effective leaders must act despite uncertainty.

**To the Lighthouse** by Virginia Woolf
A modernist masterpiece where "nothing happens" in the conventional sense, yet everything happens internally. The novel trains readers to find meaning in ambiguity and to understand that human experience resists neat narratives.

**Relevance:** Develops patience with processes that lack clear resolution—essential for long-term strategic initiatives.

### For Long-Horizon Thinking

**One Hundred Years of Solitude** by Gabriel García Márquez
This Nobel Prize-winning novel tracks seven generations of the Buendía family, showing how decisions, traits, and patterns echo across time. The cyclical nature of history and the weight of legacy are rendered vividly.

**Relevance:** Executives learn to think generationally about organizational culture, succession, and institutional memory.

**War and Peace** by Leo Tolstoy
Tolstoy's epic spans the Napoleonic Wars, depicting how individual choices intersect with historical forces. The novel interrogates whether great leaders shape history or are swept along by it.

**Relevance:** Challenges executives to consider the limits of agency and the role of systemic forces in outcomes.

### For Second-Order Consequences

**Anna Karenina** by Leo Tolstoy
Tolstoy examines how a single transgression—an affair—radiates outward, affecting families, social standing, and ultimately survival. The novel's famous opening line ("All happy families are alike; each unhappy family is unhappy in its own way") signals its focus on complexity.

**Relevance:** Demonstrates how private decisions create public consequences—essential learning for executives whose personal and professional lives intersect.

**Crime and Punishment** by Fyodor Dostoevsky
Raskolnikov commits murder believing himself above conventional morality, only to discover that psychological and social consequences cannot be escaped through rationalization. The novel is a meditation on hubris and accountability.

**Relevance:** A warning against the arrogance of believing one can calculate all variables; executives learn humility about their predictive models.

### For Understanding Human Nature

**The Great Gatsby** by F. Scott Fitzgerald
Fitzgerald's critique of the American Dream explores ambition, illusion, and self-destruction. Gatsby's relentless pursuit of an impossible goal despite warning signs mirrors many failed business ventures.

**Relevance:** Executives learn to distinguish vision from delusion and to recognize when sunk costs should be abandoned.

**Invisible Man** by Ralph Ellison
Ellison's masterpiece explores identity, race, and the experience of being unseen by those in power. The protagonist's journey through institutions that refuse to recognize his humanity offers insights into organizational blindspots.

**Relevance:** Essential reading for understanding diversity, inclusion, and the voices that organizations systematically ignore.

### For Systems Thinking

**Middlemarch** by George Eliot
Often called the greatest novel in the English language, Middlemarch depicts an entire community—its politics, marriages, ambitions, and failures. Eliot shows how individual choices ripple through social networks.

**Relevance:** A masterclass in stakeholder mapping and understanding how organizational ecosystems function.

**The Magic Mountain** by Thomas Mann
Set in a sanatorium in the Swiss Alps, Mann's novel explores how isolated environments develop their own logic, cultures, and pathologies. Time becomes distorted; priorities shift; the outside world fades.

**Relevance:** A metaphor for organizational insularity and the dangers of echo chambers in corporate culture.

## 📚 Implementation: Building a Literary Practice

### Start Small
Begin with shorter works: novellas like Dostoevsky's Notes from Underground or Tolstoy's The Death of Ivan Ilyich. These can be completed in a weekend yet deliver profound insights.

### Create Accountability
Form an executive book club focused on literary fiction. Discussion deepens comprehension and reveals multiple interpretations—modeling collaborative strategic analysis.

### Read Actively
Approach literature as you would a strategic challenge:
- What are the characters' unstated motivations?
- What second-order consequences are emerging?
- Where is the author withholding judgment?
- What would you do differently?

### Integrate with Development
Include literary fiction in leadership development programs alongside traditional case studies. The contrast illuminates what each form teaches.

---

## Conclusion

The most valuable skill for executives facing uncertainty is not analytical precision—it is the capacity to think clearly when analysis reaches its limits. Literary fiction develops this capacity as no business text can.

Classic novels do not offer solutions because real strategic challenges have no predetermined answers. They force tolerance for ambiguity because uncertainty is the executive's permanent condition. They train long-horizon thinking because consequences unfold over years and decades, not quarters.

Case studies teach what happened. Literature teaches how to think when you cannot know what will happen.

For executives seeking to develop strategic thinking under uncertainty, the reading list should include not just Harvard Business Review, but Dostoevsky, Tolstoy, and Eliot. The boardroom and the library are not as distant as they appear.
